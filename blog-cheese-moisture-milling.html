Moisture and Salt Monitoring in Cheddar Cheese Milling

Why the salt-in-moisture ratio set at the milling and salting step governs Cheddar ripening, how moisture and chloride are determined by the ISO | IDF reference methods, and what peer-reviewed near-infrared studies show about measuring them at-line and in-line.

Why milling and salting set the course of ripening

Cheddar is a dry-salted cheese: after cheddaring the curd is milled and, when the desired acidity is reached, salt is mixed into the milled curd before moulding and pressing, as the Codex Standard for Cheddar describes in its appendix on the method of manufacture [1]. Guinee's review describes how salt limits spoilage and pathogen growth and alters composition, microbial and enzymatic activity and the biochemistry of ripening; because many factors influence salt uptake and distribution, precise control of salting is essential for consistent quality [2]. For a dairy plant, the salting belt is the last point at which a batch's salt-to-moisture balance can be adjusted.

Salt-in-moisture: the parameter that matters

The parameter cheesemakers work with is salt-in-moisture (S/M), salt expressed as a percentage of the moisture phase in which starter bacteria and ripening enzymes act. Rulikowska et al. varied salt addition between 0.50 % and 3.00 % (w/w) in Cheddar: lower salt reduced pH, raised water activity and the growth of starter and non-starter lactic acid bacteria, and enhanced proteolysis, with adverse effects on flavour and texture [3]. Upreti et al. compared Cheddar at S/M levels of 4.8 % and 6.4 % over 48 weeks and found more proteolysis and higher non-starter counts in the low-S/M cheeses [4]. The companion paper shows how directly the salting rate fixes S/M: salting milled curd at 2.25 % or 3.5 % of curd weight, at pH 5.4, gave S/M of 4.77 % and 6.68 % respectively [5].

Moisture also has a regulatory ceiling. The Codex Standard for Cheddar sets a reference level of 48 % to 60 % milkfat in dry matter and, for cheese in that range, a minimum dry matter of 61 %, which corresponds to a maximum moisture of 39 % [1].

The reference methods and their time cost

Moisture in cheese is determined as total solids by the gravimetric oven-drying reference method ISO 5534 | IDF 4 [6]. Salt is determined as chloride by potentiometric titration according to ISO 5943 | IDF 88, applicable to cheeses containing more than 0.2 % chloride ion [7]. Both are laboratory procedures; by the time the figures are available, the curd that produced the sample has normally been pressed. They remain the basis of every specification and calibration, but cannot steer the salting belt while a batch is on it.

What near-infrared studies on cheese show

Near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy has been applied to Cheddar moisture for decades. Wehling and Pierce, with a three-wavelength filter instrument, obtained r² 0.92 and a standard error of performance (SEP) of 0.38 % against the oven method on 47 validation samples [8]. Sample temperature significantly affected the spectra, so samples had to be equilibrated to a uniform temperature, and ageing may also change the NIR characteristics of cheese [8]. In a later comparison, PLS regression on first-derivative reflectance spectra of grated samples gave an SEP of 0.335 % for moisture, whereas a fibre-optic probe on intact blocks was less reliable [9]. Margolies and Barbano reported that the NIR instruments in four cheese factories gave SEP values of 0.30 for moisture and 0.45 for fat on 36 validation Cheddar samples [10].

Salt is the harder parameter. In 64 processed cheese samples with inorganic salts between 2.0 % and 4.7 % (w/w), Blazquez et al., whose best moisture and fat models used scatter correction and a second derivative, found salt prediction less accurate [11]. In the Cheddar factories studied by Margolies and Barbano, salt was measured coulometrically rather than by NIR, and a mid-infrared transmittance method on dissolved cheese reached an SEP of 0.036 against 0.139 for the coulometric method at a mean salt content of 1.8 % [10]. The practical reading: NIR moisture in cheese is well established, an NIR salt calibration must be validated on the plant's own curd, and an S/M value computed from two measurements carries the error of both.

From the sample cup to the salting belt

Solberg et al. implemented in-line NIR in large-scale production of a Gouda-type cheese in place of laboratory grab sampling: the continuous signal gave more precise batch estimates and revealed sources of process variation that grab sampling could not have discovered [13]. Three practices keep such an installation defensible:

  • Calibrate on curd as it is presented. Published Cheddar calibrations used grated, temperature-equilibrated cheese, and probe readings on intact blocks were less reliable [8][9]; a model for loose milled curd at belt temperature must be built on that material.
  • Anchor the calibration to the reference methods. NIR values are only as good as the ISO 5534 and ISO 5943 results they are regressed against [6][7], and validation should follow the ISO 21543 | IDF 201 guidelines for NIR on milk products [12].
  • Use the moisture value as a control input. Because the salting rate sets S/M for a given curd moisture [5], a reading taken before salting lets the salt dose be adjusted batch by batch.
