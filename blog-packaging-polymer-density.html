Density and Crystallinity Grading of Polyethylene Packaging

How density and crystallinity of polyethylene packaging grades are determined by the reference methods, what the near-infrared spectrum of polyethylene actually encodes, and what peer-reviewed NIR calibrations for density have achieved on pellets, film and melt.

Why density is the grading parameter

Polyethylene packaging film and the resin it is made from are specified by density, and density in polyethylene is largely a statement about crystallinity: the test standards describe the material as a composite in which high-density crystalline regions are connected by lower-density amorphous material, the ratio of the two phases setting the final density [1]. Low-density (LDPE), linear low-density (LLDPE) and high-density (HDPE) grades differ in chain branching and in how it is accommodated; HDPE and LDPE crystal lattices contain very few defects, LLDPE incorporates its comonomer units in the lattice, and the chain ends and branch junctions of LDPE are excluded from the crystal into a thick amorphous layer [2]. For a converter in the chemical industry, confirming that an incoming lot is the grade named on the certificate is therefore a density and crystallinity question, and density is the property the standards recommend for following physical changes in a material and for identifying it [3].

What the reference methods measure

ASTM D1505 places a specimen in a liquid column whose density increases uniformly from top to bottom and reads the density from the level at which the specimen settles against standards of known density; the method is stated to be equivalent to ISO 1183-2 and designed to be accurate to better than 0.05 % [3]. ISO 1183-1 specifies immersion, liquid-pycnometer and titration methods for non-cellular plastics, including powders, flakes and granules [4], and ASTM D792 covers displacement in water or other liquids [5]. ASTM D4883 instead passes ultrasound through the sample and exploits the distinct response of the two phases; because inorganic fillers raise the gravimetric density but have little or no effect on the ultrasonic result, the standard describes its output as basically a base-resin density, with a stated precision of ±0.08 % or better [1]. Crystallinity itself comes from differential scanning calorimetry, for which ASTM D3418 and ISO 11357-3 define the transition temperatures and the enthalpies of melting and crystallisation [6][7], and the melt mass-flow rate of ISO 1133-1 is the usual companion to density on a resin certificate [8].

Two cautions in the standards carry over to any spectroscopic shortcut. ASTM D792 notes that portions of one sample can differ in density because of differences in crystallinity, thermal history, porosity and composition [5], and ASTM D3418 warns that true heats of fusion are to be determined in conjunction with a structural investigation [6]. A density value is tied to the specimen's form and processing history, not only to the resin that was delivered.

What NIR actually measures in polyethylene

There is no density band in the near-infrared spectrum. What polyethylene shows are overtones and combinations of the C–H stretching and bending vibrations, and the physical state of the chains modulates them; Lachenal noted that degree of crystallinity, orientation and water content of polymers can be read from NIR spectra [9]. Mizushima et al. assigned the polyethylene bands in the 1,650–1,900 nm region on the basis of data for normal alkanes, attributed the 1,690 and 1,710 nm bands to the CH3 groups of branches and of chain ends respectively, and followed the conformational changes of HDPE, LLDPE and LDPE through melting [2].

This is why NIR density calibrations work, and also what they rest on. Shimoyama et al. measured diffuse-reflectance spectra of pellets of 16 LLDPE grades with densities of 0.911–0.925 g/cm³, obtained PLS correlation coefficients of 0.961–0.970 with a standard error of prediction of 0.001 g/cm³ for raw, second-derivative and MSC-treated spectra alike, and found that bands at 1,192, 1,376 and 1,698 nm due to overtone and combination modes of the CH3 groups carried the density information [10]. The model, in other words, reads the short-chain branch content that governs how much of the chain can crystallise. Sato et al. extended this to 18 samples spanning HDPE, LDPE and LLDPE: principal component analysis of MSC-treated spectra separated the three families, PLS models predicted density, crystallinity and melting point, and the density model gave correlation coefficients of 0.9872–0.9928 with RMSECV of 0.0018–0.0023 g/cm³ almost regardless of whether spectra were recorded at 2, 4, 8 or 16 cm−1 resolution, while only the melting-point model benefited from higher resolution [11]. The same study built density models from transmission spectra of thin films, the form that matters for packaging [11].

From pellet to melt to film

The same chemistry has been used on the production line. Watari et al. installed an FT-NIR spectrometer with a purpose-built sampling cell on molten LLDPE in a real plant, collected calibration samples for three months and then monitored density continuously for a further three months; the on-line standard error of prediction was ±2.1 mg/cm³ over a range of 0.91–0.95 g/cm³ [12]. Rohe et al. built a transmission sensor with fibre-optic probes and an all-quartz measuring cell for extruders, operated it at up to 300 °C and 37 MPa, and reported deviations between predicted and actual blend composition in the range of ±0.25 % [13]. Three practices follow:

  • Match the sample form. Pellets in diffuse reflectance, film in transmission and melt in a heated cell give different spectra of the same resin, and the published models were built separately for each form [10][11][12].
  • Calibrate within a grade family and span the range. The LLDPE pellet models covered 0.911–0.925 g/cm³ [10] and the plant model 0.91–0.95 g/cm³ [12]; a lot outside the calibration population, or a filled or pigmented compound whose gravimetric density no longer tracks the base resin [1], should be flagged by outlier diagnostics rather than predicted.
  • Keep the reference methods in the loop. The NIR value inherits the uncertainty of the density-gradient and DSC results it was trained on [3][6], and the thermal history of the specimen affects both the reference value and the spectrum [5].
