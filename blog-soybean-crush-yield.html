Optimizing Oil and Protein Extraction in Soybean Crush Plants

How solvent extraction and desolventising-toasting determine the residual oil, moisture and protein quality of soybean meal, which AOCS reference methods and trading specifications define them, and where near-infrared measurement can and cannot support control — based on the technical and peer-reviewed literature.

What sets oil recovery and meal quality in a crush plant

Soybean crushing in the food and feed industry produces two saleable streams, crude oil and meal, from the same bean. Solvent extraction is the most common process used to recover oil today and comprises, after seed preparation and flaking, oil extraction, meal desolventising, meal drying and cooling, miscella distillation and solvent recovery [1]. Hexane is the dominant extraction solvent, and a comprehensive review of more than 70 solvents described in the literature notes that the choice of solvent depends on which primary end product, oil or meal, is to be optimised [2].

The meal is traded on composition. The NOPA trading rules give typical specifications of 44.0–49.0 % protein, 0.5 % fat, a maximum of 3.5 % fibre and a maximum of 12.0 % moisture, all of which can be modified by agreement between buyer and seller [3]. Under the same rules a lot within 0.5 % below the protein specification is not penalised, and larger protein deficiencies are settled at two times the delivered invoice price per unit of protein [3]. Protein and moisture therefore decide the commercial value of each lot.

The desolventiser-toaster: one vessel, several objectives

After extraction the solvent-wet flakes pass to the desolventiser-toaster (DT). Its main purpose is to separate hexane from the meal; it must recover as much hexane as possible and produce meal of good nutritional quality with low energy consumption [4]. These objectives are coupled: in a stage-wise model of an industrial DT, Paraíso et al. examined how the hexane content of the incoming cake influences meal moisture and live-steam consumption [4]. In 48 pilot-plant runs on hexane-extracted flakes, Mustakas et al. found that meal quality improved with heating time, jacket steam pressure and moisture content, and that the moisture level during toasting was directly affected by the hexane level in the material fed to the toaster [5]. Mustakas et al. also note that both underheated and overheated meals are of inferior nutritive value [5].

Reference methods and processing indicators

Composition is defined by AOCS methods, summarised in the table below; NOPA reports constituents on the as-received basis and converted to a 12 % moisture basis [3], and the AOCS proficiency programme for unground soybean meal adds Ba 9-58 for urease activity [6].

Heat treatment in the DT is judged indirectly. Urease activity (AOCS Ba 9-58) and protein solubility in 0.2 % KOH have long been used, and the protein dispersibility index (PDI) has been proposed as an alternative [7]. Batal et al. autoclaved solvent-extracted soy flakes for increasing times and fed them to chicks: the urease index stayed high (a pH rise of 1.65 to 2.4) at short heating times and then fell suddenly to 0.3 or below, KOH solubility decreased but often inconsistently, and PDI fell steadily from above 70 % to generally below 30 %, leading the authors to conclude that PDI was the more consistent and sensitive indicator of minimum adequate heat processing [7]. PDI results depend on the method: the slow-stir AOCS Ba 10b-09 gives generally lower values than the fast-stir Ba 10-65, so the method must be stated with the result [8].

Where near-infrared measurement fits

NIR has an official role at the start of the process. AOCS Am 1-92 covers the simultaneous determination of oil, moisture and volatile matter, and protein in oilseeds by near-infrared reflectance, on cleaned seed or on the sample as received provided impurities do not exceed 2.0 % by mass, and it is valid only within the calibration ranges of the analyser [9]. It is a seed method; applying NIR to meal requires calibrations developed on meal. For protein-rich feed ingredients, Fontaine et al. built NIRS calibrations for amino acids, protein and moisture on more than 1,000 samples and, validating on independent soy and meat-meal samples, found that with a few exceptions 85–98 % of the amino-acid variance was explained [10]. ISO 12099 sets out how such calibrations should be developed and validated for animal feeding stuffs [11].

Processing indicators are a harder target. In a Kansas State research report on soy flakes autoclaved for 0 to 60 minutes, NIRS regression models predicted total lysine (r² 0.969) and reactive lysine (r² 0.988) from the official results; urease activity, trypsin inhibitor activity and KOH solubility were measured by the official methods only, and no NIRS models for them were reported [12]. Two practical consequences follow:

  • Separate composition from processing quality. NIR models for moisture and protein in meal rest on a substantial literature [10][11]; urease, PDI and KOH solubility should remain reference determinations unless a site-specific NIR calibration has been validated against Ba 9-58 or Ba 10b-09 [6][8][12].
  • Calibrate on the plant's own reference data. Residual oil, moisture and protein models should be built on results from the AOCS methods the trade uses [3], and their accuracy established on independent samples from the same plant, as ISO 12099 describes [11].
