Reducing Ingredient Waste in Cattle Feed Production

Why cattle feed rations are over-formulated against ingredient variability, what published studies show about the sources of that variability and what near-infrared spectroscopy can measure, and how an NIR step at intake and at the mixer fits into a feed mill's routine.

Why feed is over-formulated

Rations for dairy and beef cattle are formulated on a nutrient basis: the National Academies requirement systems express what the animal needs as crude protein, fibre fractions, starch and energy, and ingredients are chosen to supply those nutrients at least cost [1][2]. In the feed industry the declared constituents of a compound feed are also subject to official control, with the reference procedures — oven drying for moisture, Kjeldahl for crude protein, among others — laid down in Annex III of Commission Regulation (EC) No 152/2009 [3].

The ingredients, however, are not what the formulation table says. Buckmaster and Muller applied uncertainty analysis to total mixed rations and found that uncertainty in the nutrient concentrations of the ingredients was the largest contributor to the overall uncertainty of the delivered diet, with forage dry matter and the amounts of nutrient-dense ingredients also contributing substantially [4]. A formulator who must not fall below a declared minimum, and who does not know today's protein content of the soybean meal in the bin, adds a safety margin of the expensive ingredient. That margin is the “giveaway”: protein, fat or energy paid for but not required, and it is the quantity that measurement at the plant is meant to shrink.

Where the variability comes from

St-Pierre and Weiss partitioned the variance of nutrient composition data from commercial dairy farms into farm, day-to-day, sampling and analytical components and found that sampling alone accounted for 30 to 81 % of the within-farm variance of silages, depending on nutrient and silage type, and that sampling plus analytical variance made up roughly 40 to 70 % of the within-farm variance of total mixed rations [5]. Their conclusion was that data from a single sample are not highly reliable [5]. For a mill this means that one laboratory result per ingredient lot is a weak basis for a formulation decision: part of what looks like a change in the ingredient is sampling noise. Reducing giveaway therefore needs more measurements rather than a single more precise one, and results that arrive before the ingredient is dosed rather than after the batch has shipped.

What NIR measures in feed ingredients and mixed feed

Near-infrared spectroscopy has been applied to feed composition for half a century. Norris et al. recorded reflectance spectra of 87 ground forage samples and, using up to nine wavelengths, obtained a correlation coefficient of 0.99 for crude protein and predicted an independent half of the sample set within a standard error of ±0.95 % for crude protein [6]. Givens and Deaville reviewed the following two decades of NIRS in animal nutrition, covering forages and non-forage feeds [7], and ISO 12099 now gives guidelines for applying NIR to animal feeding stuffs and cereals, including validation against the reference methods [8].

Simmler et al. built 30 PLS models on 1,437 reference samples of poultry feed, maize, wheat, soybean and DDGS and found that most protein, fat and moisture models reached a hold-out RPD above 3, while most fibre, water-soluble carbohydrate and ash models exceeded 2, with the heterogeneity of the material co-determining the result [9]. The study concerned poultry feed, but the ingredients overlap with ruminant concentrates, and the ranking is consistent with the physics: protein, fat and water have strong near-infrared absorptions, whereas fibre and ash are predicted indirectly and less precisely.

NIR at intake and at the mixer

Two measurement points address giveaway directly. At intake, each delivery of a protein meal, grain or co-product is scanned before it enters the bin, so that the formulation runs on the measured composition of the lot rather than on a table value. At the mixer, the discharged feed is checked against the formulated target. Fernández-Ahumada et al. applied NIR equations developed for intact finished compound feeds to samples taken at the mixing stage and found predictions very similar to those for the final product (crude protein 15.65 % at the mixer against 15.87 % in the final product; crude fibre 7.69 % against 8.02 %), concluding that NIR is viable at this stage because corrections can still be made before the batch is finished [10]. Moving a calibration to a process instrument is a separate task: the same group showed that spectral standardisation between a laboratory and a diode-array process instrument considerably reduced the standard error of prediction, but acceptable accuracy required the final model to contain spectra from both instruments [11].

One nuance should be kept in view. Mixer uniformity is conventionally tested with a tracer and expressed as a coefficient of variation across samples from one batch; Clark et al. found that crude protein is a poor tracer because many ingredients contribute protein, and only synthetic amino acid markers reached a coefficient of variation below 10 % in their trials [12]. Rubio et al. compared in-line NIR with tracer methods in broiler feed and found that the coefficient of variation depended on both mixing time and method [13]. An NIR protein reading at the mixer exit is therefore a check on macro-composition and dosing, not a substitute for a tracer test of micro-ingredient distribution. Three practices follow:

  • Measure each ingredient lot, not each ingredient. Sampling is a major variance component [5]; several sub-samples per delivery, formulated on their mean, let the safety margin be narrowed with numbers to justify it.
  • Keep the reference methods in the loop. NIR calibrations are built on, and periodically checked against, the Regulation 152/2009 procedures [3] as ISO 12099 describes [8].
  • Validate the process calibration on the process instrument. Laboratory models do not transfer unchanged to an inline sensor [11].

Where USTECH fits

At intake and final control the USTECH MasterLine17EC at-line analyzer is used for raw materials, animal feed and forage. At the mixer discharge the ProLine17EC and ProLine17ECS inline NIR analyzers mount on chutes and conveyors, and ProChem passes their results to the PLC or SCADA system for dashboards, alarms and setpoint adjustment.
