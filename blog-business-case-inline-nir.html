How to Build the Business Case for Inline NIR

A method, not a promise: which value levers an inline NIR analyzer can move, how to quantify each one from your own process data using process-capability and target-setting statistics, and which costs and risks belong on the other side of the ledger — with the quality-engineering and process-analytical literature behind each step.

What the business case has to show

A request for an inline analyzer in the food and feed industry or in chemical and pharmaceutical production competes with other capital projects and needs numbers that belong to the plant. The FDA's process analytical technology (PAT) guidance lists where the gains are likely to come from — shorter cycle times through on-, in- or at-line measurement and control, fewer rejects, less scrap and re-processing, real-time release, more automation and fewer human errors, better use of energy and materials, and more capacity — and states explicitly that these gains will vary with the process and the product [1]. A business case is therefore a calculation on your own data, lever by lever. Where the costs of the existing laboratory routine arise is discussed in a separate article on the hidden costs of lab-based quality control.

Lever one: variability, target setting and giveaway

The largest lever is usually the margin between the process target and its specification limit. Begin with a capability study on historical data: the indices Cp, CPU, CPL and Cpk relate the spread and the centring of the process to its tolerances and can be used with one-sided limits such as a protein minimum or a moisture maximum [2][3]. Hunter and Kartha showed that the further the target is set on the safe side, the fewer items fall outside the limit but the higher the give-away cost, with the most profitable target depending on the process variability [4]; Golhar gave a simple linear expression for the optimum process mean when underweight units must be reprocessed [5]. For packed goods the regulator fixes part of the problem: Directive 76/211/EEC requires that the average content is not less than the nominal quantity and limits the proportion of packages below the tolerable negative error, so the target a packer must run depends on its standard deviation [6].

The method follows directly. Estimate the current mean and standard deviation of the critical attribute from your own records, compute the target your current spread requires, then repeat the calculation with the spread you expect under closer measurement and control; the value is the difference in target multiplied by throughput and by the cost of the constituent or the energy involved. Two corrections keep this honest. First, the observed variance is the sum of the process variance and the measurement-system (gauge) variance, so a gauge capability study of the analyzer itself belongs in the calculation [3]. Second, a faster measurement reduces variability only if someone or something acts on it; the value comes from process monitoring and control, of which the analyzer is one part [7].

The other levers, quantified the same way

Each remaining lever in the FDA list maps to a record the plant already keeps [1]. Rejects and re-processing: count the off-spec batches or lots over a representative period, with their disposition cost, and estimate which of them an earlier result would have allowed to be corrected. Release time: measure how long product waits for a laboratory result and what the wait costs. Laboratory workload: list the routine samples per shift the analyzer could cover, while keeping the reference analyses needed to build and check the calibration [8]. Energy: where a drying or evaporation step is run to a conservative moisture target, the target-setting models developed for fill quantities [4][5] can be adapted to the moisture specification. Wider uses of process data, such as fault diagnosis [7], are harder to price and better listed than counted.

The cost side

Beyond the purchase price, the main items are the sample interface and installation, integration into the control system, and calibration. A multivariate calibration needs a calibration set of samples with reference values that spans the variation expected in use, followed by validation and ongoing checks of performance [8]; each of these consumes reference analyses and analyst time. Calibrations also need maintenance when raw materials, the process or the instrument change, and Workman notes that obtaining identical results from several instruments with one calibration still eludes technologists, so replacing or adding a unit is a project in its own right [9]. These costs recur and belong in the case as annual figures.

Risks to put in the case

  • The measurement may not represent the stream. The Theory of Sampling treats sample selection as an error source in its own right, and an inline probe sees only part of a moving stream [10]; validate the interface against representative reference samples before trusting the variance figures.
  • Published on-line evidence is thinner than it looks. Porep et al. caution that many studies claiming on-line NIR application in food production were in fact laboratory work [11]; benefits should be estimated from trials on the actual line.
  • Count only the variance reduction you will act on. A target shift is justified by the measured process distribution under the new control scheme [3][4], not by the presence of a faster instrument.
