Bioreactor Feeding Optimization in Mammalian Cell Culture

Why glucose and lactate control matters in mammalian (CHO) fed-batch culture, why near-infrared spectroscopy is challenging in aqueous culture media, and what the published work on in-situ NIR, Raman and mid-infrared monitoring and feedback feeding actually shows.

Why feeding control matters in CHO culture

Therapeutic proteins in the pharmaceutical industry are largely produced in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cell cultures fed with glucose [1]. In the usual fed-batch mode, glucose is delivered as a large bolus at set time points chosen from prior process knowledge and off-line analysis; Esmonde-White et al. describe this practice as labour- and resource-intensive, note that sampling increases the risk of contamination, and conclude that it does not adequately control glucose and lactate [1]. Lactate matters because its accumulation often impairs culture performance and the production of the therapeutic protein [2]. The FDA's 2004 PAT guidance supplies the regulatory frame: a system for designing, analysing and controlling manufacturing through timely measurements of critical quality and performance attributes of raw and in-process materials and processes [3]. In-situ spectroscopy is the obvious candidate for such measurements, but the choice of technique is not neutral.

Why NIR is challenging in culture media

Near-infrared absorptions are overtones and combinations of C–H, N–H and O–H fundamentals; the bands are broad and strongly overlapped, and in submerged processes the spectrum is dominated by the overwhelming presence of water, with the region around 1450 nm completely covered by the first O–H overtone [4]. Glucose, lactate, glutamine and ammonia sit at gram-per-litre or millimolar levels on top of that water background and alongside many other medium components.

It can nevertheless be done. Arnold et al. placed a steam-sterilisable in-situ fibre-optic NIR probe in a CHO-K1 culture and built models for glucose, lactate, glutamine and ammonia with standard errors of prediction of 0.072 g/L, 0.0144 g/L, 0.308 mM and 0.036 mM, respectively; the calibration used large data sets so that all likely variation in stoichiometry was included [5]. Rhiel et al. identified a second, subtler problem: during a cultivation the analytes change together, because metabolism consumes nutrients and produces by-products, so a calibration built on culture samples can learn that covariance rather than the analyte itself [6]. Their adaptive calibration removed the metabolism-induced covariance, used the 4800–4200 cm-1 combination region, and reached standard errors of prediction of 0.82 mM for glucose and 0.94 mM for lactate in undiluted serum-based medium, measured off-line [6].

Where Raman and mid-infrared are used

Abu-Absi et al. reported the technical feasibility of an in-line Raman probe in a mammalian cell culture bioreactor, predicting glutamine, glutamate, glucose, lactate, ammonium, viable cell density and total cell density simultaneously [7]. Because NADH and flavins in CHO cells autofluoresce in the visible range, bioprocess Raman is run with near-infrared excitation at 785 or 830 nm [1]. Raman has since been used to close the feeding loop. Berry et al. built a PLS glucose model from spiking studies and eight bench-scale calibration batches and fed glucose on demand; holding glucose at a low constant target reduced product glycation from about 9 % to 4 %, whereas a stepwise-decreasing target did not reduce it relative to bolus feeding [8]. Matthews et al. fed glucose only when Raman-predicted lactate was below a setpoint (4.0 or 2.5 g/L) and glucose was below 0.5 g/L; their PLS models had RMSEP of 0.27 g/L for glucose and 0.20 g/L for lactate, culture duration rose from 11 to 13 days, and harvest titre increased by 85 % over the historical process [2]. These are results for specific processes and cell lines, not general expectations.

Mid-infrared measures fundamental vibrations rather than overtones. Rhiel et al. monitored glucose and lactate in situ in CHO bioreactor cultures with PLS models that were applied unchanged after 2.3 years, with standard errors of estimate of 1 to 3 mM for lactate; the glucose model showed a constant offset that had to be adjusted before inoculation, and the detector required daily liquid-nitrogen filling [9]. Reviews of mammalian cell culture monitoring list NIR, mid-infrared, 2D fluorescence and dielectric capacitance among the in-situ techniques already tested, each relying on chemometric methods such as PCR, PLS or neural networks to extract the information [10].

From prediction to feed control

Whatever the technique, the spectrum becomes a feeding decision only through a chemometric model, and the review literature treats wavelength selection, pre-processing, principal component analysis and PLS as the core steps [11]. Three practical points follow from the studies above:

  • Design the calibration to break metabolic covariance. Spiking experiments and batches deliberately run over a wide concentration range give the model independent variation in the target analyte [6][8].
  • Decide the control logic before the model. The feeding rule determines which analyte must be predicted and at what level; Matthews et al. needed both glucose and lactate predictions to restrict feeding [2].
  • Validate for the intended use. ASTM E2898 gives a risk-based approach to validating analytical methods for PAT applications, so the validation effort matches the role the prediction plays in control [12].
