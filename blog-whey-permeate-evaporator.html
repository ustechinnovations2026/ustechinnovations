Total Solids Control in Whey Permeate Evaporators

How falling-film evaporators concentrate whey and permeate, why the outlet total solids governs fouling, lactose crystallisation and drying, and what the peer-reviewed literature and dairy standards say about measuring it in-line.

Why the outlet concentration of a whey evaporator matters

In the dairy industry whey and ultrafiltration permeate leave the separation steps as dilute streams that must be concentrated before crystallisation or drying. The Tetra Pak Dairy Processing Handbook describes falling-film evaporator designs that concentrate whey from a solids content of 6 % to 32 %, and lists typical solids contents after evaporation of 58–65 % for whey and 70–75 % for permeate [1]. The concentrate leaving the last effect is the feed for lactose crystallisation and spray drying, and the handbook specifies each of these downstream steps for a particular dry-matter range [1][2].

Falling-film evaporation and what limits it

In a falling-film evaporator the product flows downwards as a film on the inner surface of vertical tubes while steam condenses on the outside; gaps in the film cause local overheating, the product sticks and heat transfer and cleaning suffer [1]. How far the concentration can be pushed is set by product properties such as viscosity and heat stability [1]. In New Zealand evaporators concentrating skim milk, whole milk and buttermilk, Jebson and Iyer measured heat-transfer coefficients of 0.3 to 3.2 kW/m² per °C and found that the viscosity of the feed and the momentum of the vapour were the main factors controlling them [3]; the data concern milk products, not whey.

For whey and permeate the mineral fraction adds a second limit. Tanguy et al. note that the mass deposited on evaporator tubes during whey concentration depends mainly on the mineral composition of the whey, which varies considerably with the production process [4]. Concentrating ultrafiltration permeates made from milk acidified to pH 4.6–6.3 with different acids gave either no precipitation or precipitation of calcium phosphate and calcium citrate, behaviour that could be assessed from the ions present, their anionic forms at the concentrate pH and the solubility limits of the calcium salts [4].

Lactose crystallisation and the dryer

Wong and Hartel review how crystalliser design, processing parameters and impurities govern lactose crystallisation from whey solutions, and conclude that the industrial process is still far from optimised [5]. In lactose manufacture the whey or ultrafiltration permeate is first evaporated to 60–62 % dry matter and then transferred to crystallisation tanks [2]. For powders, whey concentrate is usually evaporated, flash-cooled and crystallised to form small lactose crystals, because this gives a reduced-hygroscopic product that does not go lumpy when it absorbs moisture; crystallised permeate is fed to the spray dryer after around 4–6 h [2].

O’Shea et al. add that monitoring concentrate viscosity helps avoid pump blockage, limit fouling while maximising water removal in evaporation, and avoid nozzle fouling in spray drying [6].

Measuring total solids: reference and in-line options

ISO 6731 | IDF 21 specifies the reference oven-drying method for the total solids of milk, cream and evaporated milk [7]. Whey and permeate concentrates fall outside its stated scope, so the plant’s reference drying method for them has to be defined and documented.

Floren et al. found that digital and optical Brix refractometer readings were highly correlated with total solids in milk-replacer solutions of approximately 5.5 to 18 % total solids, but that 1.08 to 1.47 had to be added to the Brix reading to estimate total solids [8]. Bista et al. evaluated an in-line acoustic flowmeter at laboratory scale on milk protein concentrate at 45 °C over 4–21 % total solids: acoustic impedance could be related to protein and total solids content with R² above 0.96, but the relationship depended on the protein-to-total-solids ratio [9]. In both cases the conversion to total solids is composition-specific, and neither study covered whey or permeate.

Pu et al. review how near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy, used off-line on dairy products since the 1970s, has become an on-line and in-line process analytical technology, covering in-line sampling, calibration development and installation [10]. ISO 23291 | IDF 248 gives guidelines for in-line and on-line infrared spectrometry in dairy processing, including the determination of protein, fat and total solids in liquid milk and milk products by mid- and near-infrared spectrometry [11].

Closing the loop on concentrate solids

Bakker et al. focused the development of a two-effect dairy falling-film evaporator on control of the concentrate total solids and reported improved cascade control compared with earlier models [12]. Three practices follow:

  • Anchor the in-line signal to a documented reference. Check it periodically against the plant’s drying method on its own concentrate; a conversion from another product does not transfer [7][8].
  • Treat whey streams as different products. Mineral composition varies with production and governs deposits [4], and composition changes sensor response [9]; validate sweet whey, acid whey and permeate separately.
  • Measure where the control decision is made. The outlet of the final effect feeds crystallisation and drying [2], and viscosity there affects pumping and atomisation [6]; measuring before the crystalliser allows correction in time.
