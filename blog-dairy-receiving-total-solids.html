Incoming Milk Fat and Total Solids Grading at Receiving Docks

Why the composition of a raw-milk delivery, not its volume, determines what the processor pays, how the reference methods and mid-infrared analysers establish fat, protein and total solids, and what the peer-reviewed literature shows near-infrared spectroscopy can measure in unhomogenised raw milk.

Why composition decides the value of a tanker

In the dairy industry a tanker of raw milk is not bought by the litre alone. Under the United States Federal Milk Marketing Orders, for example, class prices are built from separately announced component prices for butterfat, protein, other solids and nonfat solids, and the Class III skim-milk price is defined as a multiple of the protein price plus a multiple of the other-solids price [1]. Wherever such a multiple-component scheme is in force, the fat, protein and solids results on each delivery translate directly into money paid to the supplier and into the yield the processor can expect. The receiving dock is the first point at which a compositional result has a financial consequence, so the questions are which method produces it, how it is anchored to the reference methods, and how quickly it is available.

The reference methods behind every payment result

Three reference methods define what fat, protein and total solids mean in milk. Fat is determined gravimetrically after solvent extraction according to ISO 23318 | IDF 249, which covers raw, reduced-fat, skimmed and processed liquid milk as well as cream and dried milk products [2]. Protein is derived from total nitrogen by the Kjeldahl procedure of ISO 8968-1 | IDF 20-1, which also specifies the crude-protein calculation [3]. Total solids are the residue after oven drying according to ISO 6731 | IDF 21 [4]. None of these laboratory procedures returns a result while a tanker waits at the bay; their role at receiving is indirect but decisive, because they supply the reference values against which any rapid instrument is calibrated and checked.

Mid-infrared: the established routine method for payment testing

The rapid method dairy laboratories have standardised on for payment testing is mid-infrared (MIR) spectrometry. Lynch et al. describe MIR analysers as a rapid and cost-effective means of determining milk composition for payment, note that they measure fat, protein and lactose and thereby enable the calculation of total solids, solids-not-fat and other solids, and stress that all MIR analysers are secondary instruments requiring calibration by chemical reference methods; the same paper lists the precalibration checks applied by the USDA Federal Milk Market Administrators, from homogenisation efficiency and linearity to intercorrection factors [5]. The guidelines for applying the technique to raw milk, processed milk, cream and whey are laid down in ISO 9622 | IDF 141, which limits it to products that can be pumped through the analyser's flow system [6].

Two nuances from the MIR literature carry over to any spectroscopic payment instrument. Milk fat is not a single analyte: Kaylegian et al. showed that the difference between the MIR fat reading and the reference chemistry shifts systematically with the chain length and unsaturation of the milk fatty acids, so a calibration built on one milk population can read differently on milk with a different fatty-acid profile [7]. And analyser certification is formalised: ICAR's guidelines distinguish laboratory, at-line and in-line analysers, apply ISO 9622 | IDF 141 to the MIR determination of fat, protein, lactose and urea, require that a reference method be an internationally standardised method, and list gravimetric fat determination according to ISO 23318 among the reference methods [8].

What NIR studies report for raw-milk composition

Near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy has been studied for unhomogenised raw milk mainly as an on-line tool for the milking process, and these studies are the relevant evidence for an at-line measurement at receiving. Tsenkova et al. recorded transmittance spectra of 258 unhomogenised milk samples from 400 to 2,500 nm at thicknesses of 1, 4 and 10 mm and found that spectral region and sample thickness were significant factors for fat and total protein but not for lactose; the best accuracy came from the 1,100–2,400 nm region with a 1 mm thickness and first-derivative pre-treatment [9]. Šašić and Ozaki analysed 100 raw-milk samples in the 800–1,100 nm region by PLS regression and reported a correlation coefficient of 0.996 and an RMSEP of 0.087 wt % for fat in their better prediction run, adequate results for protein and limited prediction ability for lactose [10].

Instrumented systems confirm the pattern. Kawasaki et al. built a 600–1,050 nm NIR system into an automatic milking system and, on a validation set of unhomogenised milk, obtained r² and SEP of 0.95 and 0.25 % for fat, 0.72 and 0.15 % for protein and 0.83 and 0.26 % for lactose [11]. Aernouts et al. compared visible/NIR reflectance and transmittance of raw milk: reflectance gave R² above 0.95 for fat and crude protein but below 0.75 for lactose, transmittance gave R² above 0.90 for fat and crude protein and 0.88 for lactose, neither mode predicted urea acceptably, and the 1 mm path required for transmittance was noted to complicate on-line use [12]. Guidelines for applying NIR to milk and milk products are given in ISO 21543 | IDF 201 [13]. The consistent reading is that fat is the parameter NIR measures most readily in raw milk, protein is measurable but weaker, lactose depends on the measurement mode, and the accuracy a payment scheme requires has to be demonstrated against the reference methods [2][3][4] on the plant's own milk.

Putting a spectroscopic step at the receiving dock

Whether the instrument is MIR or NIR, the practices that make a result defensible for payment are the ones the standards and evaluation protocols already describe:

  • Anchor the calibration to the reference methods. A rapid analyser is a secondary instrument [5]; its fat, protein and total-solids calibrations should be built and periodically checked against ISO 23318, ISO 8968-1 and ISO 6731 results on the plant's own milk [2][3][4].
  • Control the measurement conditions. Path length and sample presentation significantly affect fat and protein results in unhomogenised milk [9][12]; the sampling port, temperature and cell used at the dock should match those used when the calibration was built.
  • Watch for population shifts. Because the fatty-acid profile influences the infrared fat reading [7], seasonal, breed or feeding changes in the supply base call for re-validation against fresh reference samples.
