Standardizing Moisture in Milk Powder Spray Drying

Why residual moisture is the decisive control variable in milk-powder spray drying, what the glass-transition and dryer-control literature says about the sticky boundary and the available manipulated variables, and where an at-line or inline NIR moisture measurement enters the loop.

Why outlet moisture is the number that matters

In dairy spray drying, residual moisture is the powder property the operator can move most directly from the control room. The Codex standard for milk powders sets a maximum water content of 5 % (m/m) for whole, partly skimmed and skimmed milk powder, excluding the water of crystallisation of lactose [1], and the reference method for checking it is the oven method of ISO 5537 | IDF 26 [2]. Typical values quoted for commercial powders are 2.8–3.5 % for whole milk powder and 3.5–4.0 % for skim milk powder [3]. Below the target, water removed is product not sold and heat wasted; above it, the powder sticks, cakes and deteriorates faster.

Stickiness, caking and shelf life: the glass transition

A small moisture excess has large effects because the lactose in spray-dried milk powder is amorphous. Roos and Karel showed on sugar models that water lowers the glass transition temperature (Tg) of amorphous sugars and that the empirical sticky point lies slightly above Tg [4]; Jouppila and Roos found that milk powders containing lactose have Tg values close to those of pure lactose, falling as water content rises, and that lactose crystallises above a critical storage humidity [5]. For skim milk powder, Hennigs et al. found that the sticky boundary over 25–95 °C follows the predicted glass transition of lactose shifted upward by 23.3 K [6], and Ozmen and Langrish measured a Tg of 87.7 °C at 1.65 g water per 100 g dry powder and 46.7 °C at 4.52 g/100 g, virtually the same as the sticky-point temperature [7].

The same transition governs storage. Thomsen et al. stored whole milk powder with 4.4 % water (water activity 0.23, Tg 48 °C) and observed that lactose crystallisation released bound water, raising the water activity to 0.46, with concomitant surface browning [8]. A powder that leaves the dryer too wet carries a lower Tg into the bag, and the margin to storage temperature decides whether it cakes and browns within its shelf life [5][8].

What the dryer operator can actually move

In a two-stage plant the chamber dries the powder to about 4 % above the final moisture and a fluid bed removes the rest, which lets the outlet air temperature run 15–20 °C lower than in single-stage drying and saves about 10 % of the energy [3]. The classical control structure manipulates the concentrate supply to the atomiser to hold the outlet air temperature at its setpoint, outlet temperature being the closest available proxy for powder moisture [3]. Inlet air temperature, concentrate total solids and ambient humidity all shift the moisture obtained at a given outlet temperature, and the outlet temperature can only be raised within limits set by product quality and thermoplastic stickiness [3].

The control literature states the problem in the same terms. Petersen et al. describe the main challenge of spray-dryer control as meeting the residual-moisture specification while keeping the powder from sticking to the chamber walls [9]. On a four-stage dryer model driven by industrially recorded disturbances, the same group notes that industrial PI control holds a fixed target regardless of disturbances whereas model-predictive controllers adapt the operating point, with considerably better economic performance in simulation [10]; an earlier simulation put the cost improvement of an economically optimising nonlinear MPC over PI control at 26.7 % [9]. The lever is the same in every scheme: the closer the moisture target sits to the specification without crossing the sticky boundary, the more the dryer earns [9][10].

Where an inline NIR moisture value enters the loop

Both schemes are limited by the quality of their moisture information: outlet temperature is a proxy, and the oven reference describes powder that has already left the dryer [2]. Near-infrared spectroscopy measures water through its own absorption bands and can be mounted on the powder stream. Holroyd et al. reviewed the in- and on-line NIR systems trialled for milk powder and concluded that Fourier-transform NIR systems can reliably measure moisture, fat and protein in-line [11]. In a fluid bed dryer for pharmaceutical powders, Obregón et al. used an inline NIR moisture reading as the measured variable of a model predictive controller manipulating air flow and inlet air temperature [12]. With a continuous moisture value, the outlet-temperature setpoint can be trimmed on measured moisture instead of operator experience, and the target placed with a known margin to the sticky boundary [6][7]. Three practices keep such a loop defensible:

  • Measure where the moisture is final. In two-stage plants the chamber powder is still about 4 % wetter than the product [3], so the control-relevant measurement is on the powder leaving the last fluid bed.
  • Calibrate against the reference. The NIR model is anchored to ISO 5537 | IDF 26 results on the plant's own powders [2], following ISO 21543 | IDF 201 [13]; the companion article on milk powder analysis covers that side.
  • Keep the sticky boundary in the loop. The sticky-point line depends on both temperature and moisture [6][7], so a setpoint change is checked against powder temperature at the point of measurement, not only against the specification limit [1].
