Calibration Transfer Between NIR Spectrometers: Methods and Feasibility

Why a calibration built on one near-infrared spectrometer rarely runs unchanged on another, which standardisation and model-adjustment methods the literature describes, and how the feasibility of a transfer is assessed for a given instrument family — benchtop, inline or compact — on the basis of published studies and the relevant ASTM practices.

Why calibration transfer is a problem

A multivariate calibration for a process NIR analyzer in the chemical and pharmaceutical industry, or in food and feed, is a large investment in samples, reference analyses and modelling, so the natural wish is to build the model once and run it on every instrument. Workman defines calibration transfer as the chemometric techniques used to apply one spectral database, and the model built on it, to two or more instruments with statistically retained accuracy and precision, and notes that obtaining identical results from several instruments with one calibration still eludes technologists [1]. Transfer is not straightforward even between nominally identical instruments, because of wavelength shifts and differences in absorbance response between units [2], so a model developed on one spectrometer often cannot be used directly with spectra measured on another [3]. The transfer uncertainty has several ingredients: instrument reproducibility and repeatability, the reference values, the multivariate mathematics and the repeatability of sample presentation [1].

Three families of methods

Fearn classifies the approaches under three headings: making robust calibrations, adjusting calibrations and adjusting spectra [2]. Calibrations are made more transferable by spectral pre-treatments that reduce between-instrument variability and by pooling data from several instruments; they can be adjusted with slope and bias corrections estimated from a modest number of samples with known reference values; and under the third heading come methods such as piecewise direct standardisation and the patented procedure of Shenk and Westerhaus, which use spectra of samples measured on both instruments to match the response of the secondary instrument to the master [2].

The idea predates the chemometric names. In 1985 Shenk, Westerhaus and Templeton transferred calibration equations from one master scanning monochromator to six others using 60 sealed samples; the between-instrument standard errors of difference for forage constituents were smaller than those among laboratories for the chemical analyses, and satisfactory transfer was obtained with as few as 10 samples [4]. Their 1991 procedure for monochromators of the same model aligned the wavelength scale with a polystyrene standard and adjusted baselines with a sealed concentrate sample, and a repeatability file of spectra from different instruments and temperatures added to a modified PLS regression reduced the between-instrument error on test samples measured between 19 and 29.5 °C [5]. Wang, Veltkamp and Kowalski described direct standardisation (DS) and piecewise direct standardisation (PDS), in which a transfer set measured on both instruments yields a transformation that maps secondary spectra onto the master response, PDS using a small window of neighbouring wavelengths at a time [6].

What instrument comparisons show

The best method depends on the instruments and the application. Igne and Hurburgh compared DS, PDS, robust standardisation, slope-and-bias or bias-only correction of the predictions, and pre-treatment alone for whole-soybean models between two Foss Infratec and two Bruins OmegAnalyzerG units: models were transferable within and between brands with precision similar to or better than individual calibration, the optical methods that modify secondary spectra performed significantly worse than the others, and pre-processing with or without a simple correction of the predictions was enough to remove the absorbance differences and wavelength shifts [7]. Greensill et al., transferring a soluble-solids model between four diode-array spectrometers of the same model, found that a modified wavelet transform performed significantly better than six other standardisation techniques and on a par with model updating [8]. Hoffmann et al. used PDS, with only a few samples measured on both spectrometer types, to transfer data sets from two laboratory FT-NIR spectrometers to the format of a miniaturised handheld instrument despite large differences in spectral range and resolution [9].

Two readings follow for compact and inline spectrometers. Four units of the same diode-array model still needed standardisation before a model could be shared [8], so similarity of design does not by itself make a model portable. Where unit-to-unit differences are small, the lighter routes — pre-treatment, pooled calibration data and slope-and-bias correction [2][7] — may suffice, but that has to be shown for each instrument family by measurement, not inferred from the optical architecture.

Transfer without standard samples

Standard-based methods need the same physical samples measured on both instruments, which is difficult when the instruments are far apart or when chemically stable standards are not available [10]. Mishra et al. review the recent techniques, including standard-free concepts, and conclude that approaches not relying on standard-sample measurements hold promise for making models sharable between similar devices [10]. One example is domain-invariant PLS, which adds a regulariser that aligns the source and target distributions in the latent-variable space and can use partially labelled or unlabelled target data; Nikzad-Langerodi et al. demonstrated it on two NIR data sets [11].

Assessing feasibility for an instrument family

Because the outcome depends on the instruments, the product and the method, transfer feasibility is a measured property rather than a specification. Three steps recur in the studies above:

  • Check the instruments first. Wavelength shifts and absorbance-response differences are the root of the problem [2]. ASTM E1866 gives procedures for developing spectrophotometer performance tests, including for on-line process spectrophotometers, with univariate level zero and level one and multivariate level A and level B tests [12].
  • Compare transfer routes on real data. Measure a transfer set on both instruments and compare pre-treatment alone, slope-and-bias correction and a spectral standardisation such as PDS [7][8]; where several units exist, pooling their spectra into the calibration set or a repeatability file is an alternative to correcting each one [2][5].
  • Validate the transferred model as a new model. ASTM E1655 sets out practices for multivariate infrared and near-infrared quantitative analysis and criteria for validating calibration performance, assuming the spectrometer operates within the manufacturer's specifications [13]; the transferred model is validated on the secondary instrument with reference-analysed samples, because reference values and sample presentation contribute to the uncertainty as much as the optics do [1].

How USTECH approaches calibration transfer

USTECH calibrations are developed in the caliX chemometrics suite and run on the ProLine2550 inline FT-NIR analyzer, the ProLine17EC and ProLine17ECS inline NIR analyzers and the MasterLine17EC at-line analyzer. When a model built on one unit is to be used on another, USTECH treats it as a transfer project: the secondary instrument's performance is checked [12], a transfer set is measured on both units, the lighter and heavier routes are compared [2][7], and the transferred model is validated against reference values on the secondary unit [13]. Which route a given model needs is established in a feasibility study with your own samples.
