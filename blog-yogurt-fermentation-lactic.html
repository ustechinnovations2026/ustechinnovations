Monitoring Lactic Acid and pH in Yogurt Fermentation

Why ending acidification at the right moment is a critical decision in yogurt making, which reference methods define pH and titratable acidity, and what peer-reviewed studies report about following the fermentation with near-infrared, mid-infrared and Raman spectroscopy.

Why the fermentation end-point matters

In yogurt manufacture, heat-treated milk is inoculated with Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus, which act synergistically to convert lactose into lactic acid and lower the pH of the milk; yogurt curd formation is based on the isoelectric precipitation of casein as the pH falls [1]. The Codex Standard for Fermented Milks defines yoghurt by this symbiotic culture and sets a minimum titratable acidity of 0.6 % (m/m), expressed as lactic acid [2]. For dairy processors, deciding when to stop acidification is therefore both a texture question and a compositional one.

Fermentation is ended by cooling. A review of yogurt processing describes cooling to around 5 °C once the pH reaches about 4.7–4.3, which inhibits the starter culture and prevents a further rise in acidity; rapid one-phase cooling below 10 °C gives a lower viscosity than two-phase cooling, which also limits syneresis [1]. Earlier conditions matter too: Lee and Lucey found that permeability, pore size and whey separation of yogurt gels increased with a lower inoculation rate and a higher incubation temperature (approximately 46 °C versus 40 °C) [3], and their later review relates yogurt texture to total solids, heat treatment and incubation temperature [4].

Reference measurements: pH and titratable acidity

Two reference quantities describe acidification. pH is the process value at which cooling is started [1], and in industrial yogurt production it is normally the only measurement applied in-line as a real-time monitoring signal [6]. Titratable acidity is the compositional criterion: the Codex limit is expressed as a percentage of lactic acid [2], and ISO/TS 11869 | IDF/RM 150 specifies a potentiometric method for determining the titratable acidity of natural, flavoured, fruit and drinking yoghurt, fresh cheese, buttermilk and other fermented milk products [5]. Titratable acidity, however, is a laboratory titration on a sample withdrawn from the vat.

Svendsen et al. note that producers would benefit from an in-line technique reporting more than pH, such as composition, texture or contamination [6].

What in-line near-infrared studies report

Cimander et al. fused an electronic nose, a near-infrared (NIR) spectrometer and standard bioreactor probes in laboratory yoghurt fermentations; a neural network predicted lactose, galactose and lactate, and process states such as the onset of coagulation or the harvest time, with accuracy comparable to the off-line reference assay [7]. Grassi et al. compared Fourier-transform NIR (FT-NIR) spectra, analysed by principal component analysis, with rheology, pH, titratable acidity and sugar and lactic acid concentrations, and found FT-NIR useful for real-time assessment of curd development [8]. In a follow-up of 18 fermentations with three starter cultures at 37, 41 and 45 °C, multivariate curve resolution (MCR-ALS) of in-line FT-NIR spectra resolved three profiles corresponding to the coagulation phases, and the concentration profiles depended on starter and temperature in agreement with pH and rheological results [9].

Svendsen et al. monitored yoghurt-type fermentations with in-line NIR and found that the change in viscosity strongly affects light scatter; the NIR signal reflects both scatter, related to texture, and absorption, related to biomass formation and the conversion of sugar to lactic acid [6]. Muncan et al. recorded NIR spectra (830–2500 nm) every 15 minutes over 8 hours of fermentation, with pH every 30 minutes, and related the baseline and slope changes seen with acidification to water structure, protein changes and coagulation [10]. Aktas et al. fitted the Gompertz equation to in-line FT-NIR data to locate kinetic critical points of yogurt fermentation, which came about 18 % earlier on average than those from rheology [11].

Most of these studies track the course of fermentation — coagulation onset, phases and critical points — in laboratory or pilot fermentations [8][9][10][11]. A site-specific quantitative model for pH or titratable acidity still has to be calibrated and validated on the plant's own milk, cultures and recipes.

Mid-infrared and Raman

Sivakesava et al. compared FT-MIR, NIR and FT-Raman spectroscopy for at-line measurement of biomass, glucose and lactic acid in whole-broth samples from a Lactobacillus casei fermentation; FT-MIR models performed best, while NIR and FT-Raman prediction errors were high [12]. That was a bioprocess broth, not a coagulating milk gel, so the ranking does not transfer directly to yogurt. No peer-reviewed in-line Raman study of yogurt fermentation was identified for this article.

Practical points for an acidification signal

  • Anchor to the reference methods. Check any spectroscopic model against pH and titratable acidity by ISO/TS 11869 | IDF/RM 150 [5], because the compositional limit for yoghurt is defined on titratable acidity [2].
  • Separate texture from chemistry. Because gelation changes light scatter [6] and fermentation kinetics depend on starter and temperature [9], the calibration set should span the cultures, recipes and incubation temperatures used in production.
  • Follow the NIR guidance for dairy. ISO 21543 | IDF 201 gives guidelines for applying NIR spectrometry to milk products, including calibration and validation [13].
