Optimizing Fat and Protein Standardization in Liquid Milk

What the compositional classes for drinking milk require, how a separator-based in-line standardisation unit controls cream remixing, where density-based control reaches its limits, and what the literature shows for closing the loop with direct infrared measurement of fat and protein.

What standardisation has to deliver

Raw milk reaches a dairy with a fat content that varies with breed, feeding and season; the milk leaving it must match a fixed class. In the European Union, Regulation (EU) No 1308/2013 defines standardised whole milk as at least 3.50 % (m/m) fat, semi-skimmed milk as 1.50–1.80 % and skimmed milk as not more than 0.50 %, allows the fat content to be adjusted only by removing or adding cream or milk of another class, and requires at least 2.9 % (m/m) protein in milk with 3.5 % fat [1]. The US standard of identity for milk requires not less than 3.25 % milkfat and 8.25 % milk solids not fat [2].

These targets are defined by reference chemistry, not by a process sensor: fat by the gravimetric method of ISO 23318 | IDF 249:2022, which replaced ISO 1211 | IDF 1 for liquid milk [3], and protein from Kjeldahl nitrogen under ISO 8968-1 | IDF 20-1 [4]. Neither returns a result while the milk is flowing, so the plant needs a faster signal to steer the process and the reference methods to confirm the outcome.

How an in-line standardisation unit works

The Tetra Pak Dairy Processing Handbook sets out the principle: pre-heated whole milk is separated into skim milk and cream, the cream fat content is set at the separator outlet, a calculated portion of that cream is remixed into the skim-milk stream to reach the target, and the surplus cream goes to cream treatment [5]. The proportions follow from a mass balance on fat — the rectangle calculation illustrated in the handbook — so the result is only as good as the fat values entered into it [5].

A direct in-line unit closes this loop with flow, mass-flow and temperature transmitters, control valves and a computerised controller [5]. Fat in the cream line is not measured chemically: a mass-flow transmitter measures cream density, which the controller converts to fat content through the fixed relationship between the two [5]. The handbook is plain about the limits. Control by cream flow alone is quick but gives a wrong fat content as soon as the temperature or fat content of the incoming milk changes; control by density alone returns to the set-point but reacts slowly; cascade control combines the two, and the density reading itself is affected by temperature and by entrained gas, much of which follows the cream phase [5]. For a well-tuned unit the handbook states that the repeatability standard deviation of standardised milk should be below 0.015 % fat [5].

Measuring composition in the line: mid- and near-infrared

Because the conventional loop infers fat from density and never measures protein, there is a case for a spectroscopic sensor that measures both directly on the standardised stream. Mid-infrared (MIR) analysers are the routine instruments for fat, protein and lactose in liquid milk, with application guidelines in ISO 9622 | IDF 141 [6]. Their accuracy is set by the calibration set: Kaylegian et al. showed that modified milks spanning a wider component range, with fat and protein less correlated than in producer milks, gave a confidence interval around the MIR regression line several times smaller [7]. An in-line calibration for standardised milk should likewise cover every fat class the line produces, with fat and protein varied independently.

Near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy is better suited to pipeline installation, and ISO 21543 | IDF 201 covers its application to milk [8]. Geometry matters. Tsenkova et al. found for unhomogenised milk that the best fat and protein accuracy came from transmittance at 1,100–2,400 nm with a 1 mm sample thickness, while at 700–1,100 nm a 10 mm path gave comparable accuracy for fat [9]. Aernouts et al. reported R² above 0.95 for fat and crude protein in raw milk by reflectance and above 0.90 by transmittance, noting that the 1 mm path needed for transmittance considerably complicates on-line use [10]. Kawasaki et al. ran an on-line NIR system at 600–1,050 nm inside a milking robot and obtained a validation r² of 0.95 with a standard error of prediction of 0.25 % for fat, but only 0.72 and 0.15 % for protein [11].

The study closest to the standardisation problem is by Castro-Reigía et al., who used in-line NIR predictions of the fat and protein of milk entering a yoghurt process to readjust the cream and milk powder added; their PLS models reached an R² of prediction of 0.99 for fat and 0.80 for protein with RMSEP of 0.26 and 0.16, and the strategy reduced the variability of the final product's nutritional values by more than 70 % relative to the fixed recipe [12]. There, as in the milking studies, protein is the harder parameter, so a plant should expect its protein loop to be less tight than its fat loop.

Closing the loop in practice

A spectroscopic sensor on the standardised-milk line does not replace the separator's own cream-fat control; it adds an outer loop that measures the finished product and corrects the cream remix set-point, in the same cascade logic the handbook recommends for combining flow and density signals [5]. Three practices decide whether that loop can be trusted:

  • Calibrate across the product range, against the reference methods. Reference values should come from ISO 23318 and ISO 8968-1 [3][4], and the calibration set should cover every fat class the line produces, with fat and protein decorrelated [7].
  • Fix the measurement conditions. Path length and wavelength range change what an NIR milk calibration can achieve [9][10]; the cell should sit downstream of the remix point, where the stream is fully mixed, and the line should be checked for entrained air, which the handbook notes follows the cream phase [5].
  • Verify against the reference method on a schedule. The in-line value steers the process, but the class on the label is defined by the regulation and the reference chemistry [1][2][3][4], so periodic reference analyses of the finished milk remain the evidence that the loop is on target.
