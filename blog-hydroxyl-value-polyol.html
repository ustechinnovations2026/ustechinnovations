Real-Time Hydroxyl Value Monitoring in Polyol Production

Why the hydroxyl number is the end-point variable in polyether and polyester polyol production, what the titration reference methods and the two NIR standards require, what the published NIR hydroxyl-value work shows, and why reactor temperature is the first thing an inline calibration has to deal with — with the standards and literature behind each point.

Why the hydroxyl number is the end-point variable

Polyether and polyester polyols are, with the isocyanates, the main building blocks of polyurethanes, and in the chemical industry the property that defines a polyol grade is its hydroxyl number. ASTM D4274 and ISO 14900 both state the reason: hydroxyl-number data are needed to formulate polyurethane systems, because the isocyanate stoichiometry is set against the hydroxyl content of the polyol [1][2]. Polyether polyols are built by ring-opening polymerisation of propylene oxide and/or ethylene oxide onto a polyfunctional starter, so the hydroxyl number falls as the chains grow and the batch is complete when it reaches the grade specification; the chemistry and technology of oligo-polyol fabrication are covered in Ionescu's monograph [3]. The end point is therefore an analytical decision, and the question is how quickly it can be made.

The reference methods and what they involve

The reference determinations are esterification–titration procedures. ASTM D4274 contains five test methods in which the hydroxyl groups are esterified with acetic anhydride, phthalic anhydride or imidazole-catalysed phthalic anhydride or pyromellitic dianhydride and the excess reagent is back-titrated, with methods assigned to polyester, polyether, polymer and amine-initiated polyols [1]. ISO 14900:2023 specifies two methods, A mainly for readily esterified polyether polyols and sterically hindered polyols such as sugar-based ones, B for polyether, polymer and amine-initiated polyols, which may give low results for sterically hindered polyols [2]. A third route, ASTM E1899, reacts the hydroxyl groups with p-toluenesulfonyl isocyanate and titrates potentiometrically; it covers hydroxyl numbers from 0.5 to 367, suits temperature-sensitive materials and polymer polyols, and is recommended for neutral refined products, in-process samples with acidic species requiring validation of the interference [4].

All three are batch laboratory procedures: a sample is drawn, reacted and titrated; Cho et al. describe the conventional wet analysis as taking 1–3 hours and using large amounts of reagent [5]. In a reactor that is still alkoxylating or condensing, the result describes where the batch was when the sample was taken, not where it is now.

NIR hydroxyl-number determination is standardised

Near-infrared determination of the hydroxyl number of polyols has its own standards. ASTM D6342 and its equivalent ISO 15063 describe the calibration techniques, the selection of calibration materials, data collection and treatment, and the criteria for building, evaluating and validating the model, and both state that the method is suitable for research, quality control, specification testing and process control [6][7]. Two conditions in D6342 matter for an inline installation: a calibration is generally valid only for the instrument on which it was built, and results are valid only within the hydroxyl-number range and chemical composition of the calibration set, with outlier detection following ASTM E1655, the general practice for multivariate infrared calibration [6][8].

What the published NIR work shows

Yamamoto et al. reported the simultaneous NIR determination of hydroxyl value, ethylene oxide content and primary hydroxyl content of polyether polyols, three specification parameters of an EO-tipped polyol from one spectrum [9]. Cho et al. measured the hydroxyl number of polyols in a disposable glass vial, reduced the noise introduced by the narrow aperture with a wavelet transform, and built a partial least squares model whose predictions agreed with the wet analysis [5]. Closest to the reactor, Bohórquez et al. followed the hydroxylation of epoxidised high-oleic palm oil to a natural-oil polyol at 65–95 °C by measuring hydroxyl value and oxirane oxygen content by NIR absorbance, and fitted the reaction kinetics to those data [10]. None of these is an in-reactor measurement during alkoxylation, so the end-point application has to be validated on the plant's own reactor.

Temperature: the first obstacle in a reactor loop

A polyol reactor loop is hot, and its temperature is not constant across a batch. Wülfert et al. showed on short-wave NIR spectra of ethanol/water/2-propanol mixtures measured at several temperatures that temperature changes the shape of the spectra non-linearly; local PLS models built at one temperature were best at that temperature but could not be rescued by knowing the temperature of a new sample, whereas global models with the temperature variation in the calibration set gave considerably lower prediction errors at the cost of sensitivity, and the authors concluded that temperature should be modelled explicitly [11]. Their test system was a mixture of alcohols and water, so a hydroxyl-number calibration on a hot polyol is exactly the kind of model they describe [11]. Hageman et al. reviewed the methods since proposed for temperature-robust calibration [12]. Pre-processing such as scatter correction and derivatives, reviewed by Rinnan et al., removes baseline and path-length effects but not a temperature effect [13]. Three practical consequences follow:

  • Span the loop temperature in the calibration set, or thermostat the measurement point. A model built at one temperature will not predict correctly at another [11][12].
  • Stay inside the validated range. The model is valid only within the range and composition of its calibration set, and predictions flagged as outliers by the E1655 procedures should not drive the end-point decision [6][8].
  • Match the reference method to the polyol. The NIR model cannot be better than the titrations it is trained on; use the method D4274 or ISO 14900 assigns to the polyol type, and validate the interference of acidic species in in-process samples as E1899 requires [1][2][4].

How USTECH approaches it

The USTECH ProLine2550 process FT-NIR analyzer is the inline instrument for reactors and pipelines carrying liquids such as polyols; the caliX suite provides the pre-processing and PLS tools with which a hydroxyl-number model is built from a calibration set spanning the loop temperature, following ASTM D6342, ISO 15063 and E1655 [6][7][8]; and ProChem passes the predicted hydroxyl number to the PLC or SCADA as the end-point signal, with alarms and setpoints.
