Inline Fat and Moisture Standardization in Continuous Butter Making

Why water and salt in continuous butter making are controlled to a fraction of a percent, where in the buttermaker they are set, what the reference methods involve, and what peer-reviewed sensor and near-infrared studies show about measuring them at the line.

Why water and salt are controlled so tightly

Butter is sold against a compositional definition. The Codex Standard for Butter requires a minimum milkfat content of 80 % m/m, a maximum water content of 16 % m/m and a maximum milk solids-not-fat content of 2 % m/m [1]; Regulation (EU) No 1308/2013 sets the same water and non-fat limits and a milk-fat content of not less than 80 % but less than 90 % [2]. Milk fat is the costly component, so a dairy wants to run as close to the water limit as its process variability allows — and that variability is not small. Surveying four continuous churning operations, Packard found a churn-induced standard deviation under constant adjustment of ±0.087 % for moisture and ±0.058 % for salt, against an analytical error of ±0.074 % and ±0.015 %, and butter at the printer averaging 0.11 % lower in moisture than butter leaving the churn [3].

Where composition is set in a continuous buttermaker

Most continuous buttermakers follow the Fritz principle: cream is churned, separated from buttermilk and worked in a single machine [4]. In the layout described in the Tetra Pak Dairy Processing Handbook, cream enters a cooled churning cylinder; grains and buttermilk pass through separation and squeeze-drying sections; screw-driven working sections disperse the water phase; salt may be added by a high-pressure injector; and the final working stage contains an injector for the final adjustment of the moisture content [4]. Once regulated, the handbook states, moisture deviates by less than about 0.1 % provided the characteristics of the cream remain the same, and transmitters for moisture, salt, density and temperature can be fitted in the outlet and used for automatic control [4].

Those transmitters are the whole case for an inline analyser: the injectors are dosing points, and a loop around them needs the outlet composition measured often enough and with a known delay. The qualification — provided the cream stays the same — is also why the loop is needed, since churning recovery varies over the year [4].

The reference methods

The reference methods are gravimetric and sequential. ISO 3727-1 | IDF 80-1 specifies the reference method for the moisture content of butter [5], ISO 3727-2 | IDF 80-2 the reference method for non-fat solids [6], and ISO 3727-3 | IDF 80-3 the calculation of fat content from those two results [7]; salt is determined by ISO 1738 | IDF 12 [8]. These methods define what the label terms mean, and any rapid method is calibrated and judged against them. They are laboratory procedures, so they confirm a lot after the fact but cannot steer the injectors.

What the sensor and NIR literature shows

Shiinoki et al. monitored moisture and salt on-line in a continuous salted butter-making process by microwave transmission, relating phase shift and attenuation at 3 GHz to the two contents by linear regression over about 15.5–17.5 % moisture and 1.0–2.0 % salt [9]. Near-infrared spectroscopy measures water through its two broad absorption bands near 1,440 and 1,930 nm; Büning-Pfaue notes that this suits NIR to in-line water analysis, but that hydrogen bonding and sample temperature affect the reliability of the result [10].

For butter specifically, Hermida et al. calibrated an NIR instrument on 102 samples by modified partial least squares and, on an independent set of 24 samples, obtained standard errors of validation of 0.255 for moisture, 0.071 for solids-non-fat and 0.377 for fat (R² 0.83, 0.94 and 0.72), with paired t-tests showing no significant difference from the reference methods [11]. The weaker fat statistics matter less than they look: fat in butter is obtained by calculation from moisture and non-fat solids [7], so an inline strategy controls water, and where relevant salt, directly and lets fat follow.

In-line NIR has been demonstrated on a closely related plastic fat spread: Isaksson et al. measured 36 pilot-plant batches of margarine spanning 14.28–21.91 % moisture with diffuse-transmittance fibre-optic probes in the 780–1,100 nm region and obtained a cross-validation error of 0.13 % moisture in-line, essentially the same as the 0.14 % obtained off-line [12]. Guidance on applying NIR to milk and milk products is given in ISO 21543 | IDF 201, whose 2006 edition listed moisture, fat, non-fat solids and salt in butter among its applications [13].

Putting an analyser into the water-dosing loop

Three points decide whether such a loop improves on manual adjustment:

  • Calibrate against the reference methods. The NIR model inherits the error of its training values; ISO 3727-1, -2, -3 and ISO 1738 analyses on the scanned samples are the basis of calibration and validation [5][6][7][8].
  • Control temperature and presentation. The water bands respond to temperature and hydrogen bonding [10], and butter leaving the final working stage carries a temperature and density the sensor sees with its composition [4]; build the calibration under the conditions the analyser will see in production.
  • Separate sensor noise from process variability. Packard found analytical error and churn-induced variability of the same order for moisture [3]; a loop that chases sensor noise doses water against noise, so control limits should come from a repeatability study on the line.
