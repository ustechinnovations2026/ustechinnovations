Inline Solvent Purity Monitoring in Chemical Distillation Columns

Why distillation and solvent-recovery columns are usually controlled on tray temperatures, what published work shows near-infrared spectroscopy can measure in distillate and solvent streams, and how an inline analyzer is calibrated against Karl Fischer and other reference methods and kept valid — based on the cited literature and standards.

Composition is the target, temperature is the proxy

In solvent recovery and other distillation duties in the chemical and pharmaceutical industry, the quantities that matter are product compositions: the purity of the overhead distillate, the solvent lost with the bottoms, and the water left in a recovered solvent. Skogestad's tutorial on distillation dynamics and control notes that composition measurements are often expensive, unreliable and subject to time delays, which is why columns are commonly controlled on tray temperatures, with an outer composition loop in cascade where a composition measurement is available [1]. He recommends at least three temperature sensors in each column section, so that pressure changes can be corrected for and temperatures can be used to estimate product compositions [1].

Temperature remains an indirect indicator, and the relationship becomes strongly nonlinear for high-purity products: Skogestad points out that a set-point change from 99 % to 99.9 % purity would change the initial process gain by a factor of 10 unless logarithmic compositions are used [1]. A direct composition measurement at the product draw is therefore the natural complement to temperature control, provided it is fast and reliable enough to be trusted in a loop.

What near-infrared spectroscopy measures in a solvent stream

Near-infrared absorption arises from overtones and combinations of the fundamental vibrations of O–H, C–H, N–H and S–H bonds, with intensities between 10 and 1,000 times lower than those of the fundamentals [2]. Water, alcohols and hydrocarbons all carry such bonds, and the weak absorption is what allows clear liquids to be measured in transmission: Pasquini gives typical optical paths of 1 to 50 mm, shorter towards the combination region around 2200 nm [2]. The same review explains why a single-wavelength Beer's-law calibration is rarely sufficient: changes in hydrogen-bond patterns as the relative concentrations of the species change make real mixtures deviate from linear behaviour, so multivariate calibration is used [2]. Fibre optics allow the spectrometer to sit away from the process and to multiplex several measurement points up to 100 m distant, but the long-term stability of a multiplexed system has to be demonstrated before a model is built on it [2].

What the literature shows for distillations

Pasquini and Scafi coupled a fast-scanning acousto-optic tunable filter NIR spectrometer to a distillation head through a U-shaped glass flow cell of about 150 µL fed directly by the condensate, recording spectra from 950 to 1800 nm every 5 s; they demonstrated the approach on prepared binary mixtures and on adulterated gasoline, using principal component analysis to follow the changing condensate composition [3]. Their set-up was a laboratory microdistillation without a reflux column, so it shows the measurement principle rather than column performance [3].

For solvent exchange, a routine step in pharmaceutical bulk-drug production, Tarczynski et al. placed a fibre-optic dip probe in the exchange distillation and used a PLS model to follow the percentage of the lower-boiling solvent in real time, so that the end of the exchange could be determined from the measurement [4]. At manufacturing scale, Lambertus et al. developed an on-line transmission NIR method for water at parts-per-million levels in a toluene product solution after distillation, used as a control point before a Grignard reaction; models built at the development site were applied at the manufacturing site during six runs at 250-gallon scale [5]. The benefit they emphasise is not speed alone but the reduced risk of operator exposure, because sampling of a lachrymatory and mutagenic compound was no longer needed [5]. None of these studies supports a single universal working range: the range, precision and detection limit of an NIR solvent method are properties of the specific mixture, cell and calibration.

Reference methods, calibration and validation

An inline analyzer is only as good as the laboratory method it is calibrated against. For water, ASTM E203 describes volumetric Karl Fischer titration for free water and water of hydration in most solid and liquid compounds, and advises that the coulometric technique should be considered when samples routinely contain 500 mg/kg of water or less [6]. ASTM E1064 covers coulometric Karl Fischer determination of water from 0 % to 2.0 % by mass in most liquid organic chemicals [7]. ASTM E1655 is the practice for building multivariate infrared calibrations and establishing their validity at the time of calibration, and refers ongoing validation to a separate practice such as ASTM D6122 [8]. D6122 sets out the checks for multivariate online, at-line, field and laboratory analyzers: instrument performance, applicability of the model to each sample spectrum, and agreement with the primary test method; its scope is liquid petroleum products and fuels, so for solvent streams it serves as a template rather than a formal requirement [9]. Three points deserve particular attention:

  • Span the composition range. Calibration samples must cover the range of the samples to be analysed so that predictions are interpolations rather than extrapolations [9]; a model built on mid-range mixtures should not be trusted near the purity specification unless such samples were included.
  • Handle temperature explicitly. Using short-wave NIR spectra of ethanol/water/2-propanol mixtures, Wülfert et al. showed that temperature changes the spectra nonlinearly; global models that included temperature variation gave considerably lower prediction errors than local models, at the cost of sensitivity [10]. Either thermostat the cell or include the process temperature range in the calibration set.
  • Plan for calibration transfer. Chung's review of refinery NIR applications treats calibration transfer as one of the important issues to address [11], and the Lambertus et al. method was deliberately transferred from a development site to a manufacturing site [5].
