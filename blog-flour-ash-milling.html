Continuous Ash and Protein Monitoring in Wheat Flour Milling

Why ash and protein decide how flour streams are split and blended, what the incineration and Kjeldahl reference methods involve, what published work shows near-infrared spectroscopy can and cannot do for the two parameters, and how continuous monitoring of flour streams fits into a mill — with sources.

Why ash and protein steer the mill

A roller mill collects the flour from each break and reduction passage as a separate stream, which the miller blends into straight-grade, patent or clear flours. Ash is the measure used to judge those streams: the mineral content of bran is about 20 times that of the endosperm, so flour ash is used as an indicator of how completely bran has been separated from endosperm [1]. Debranning experiments confirm the gradient; the longer wheat kernels are abraded, the lower the ash of what remains [2]. Protein is the second control variable in the food and feed industry because it sets the end use and price of the flour and because, as Dowell et al. found, most other wheat quality parameters are correlated with it [3].

The cumulative ash curve makes the link to extraction rate explicit. Sakhare and Inamdar plotted cumulative flour ash against cumulative flour yield for the four break and five reduction streams of a pilot mill: ash rose from the first to the last break and from the front to the tail reduction passages, apart from a higher first break (0.71 %) and third reduction (0.76 %), and the front reduction passages gave the most flour at the lowest ash [4]. Every stream added to a blend raises extraction and ash together, and the miller's decision is where to cut; ash is therefore described as a key indicator of flour extraction rate and grade [5].

The reference methods

Ash is determined by incinerating a weighed test portion in a muffle furnace and weighing the residue. ISO 2171 specifies the method for cereals, pulses and their milled products for human consumption, including flours, semolinas and bran-rich products [6]; ICC Standard No. 104/1 ashes the sample at 900 °C until the residue is white or nearly white and reports on dry matter, so moisture is determined separately [7]; AACC Method 08-01.01 is the basic method of the AACC collection [8]. The AACC near-infrared method itself names incineration and cooling as the time-consuming steps an NIR prediction is meant to avoid [1]. For protein, the Kjeldahl procedure of ISO 20483 determines total nitrogen and calculates crude protein from it, without distinguishing protein from non-protein nitrogen [9]. Neither procedure returns a result while the stream that produced the sample is still flowing.

What NIR measures for ash and protein — and what it does not

Near-infrared spectra of foods consist of broad, overlapping overtone and combination bands of CH, OH and NH bonds; because that chemical signal is overlaid by physical effects such as particle size, NIR is a secondary method that is calibrated against a reference method with multivariate (chemometric) models, and its accuracy is bounded by the precision of those reference methods [10]. Protein has a direct NIR signature in the NH absorptions of the peptide bond, and AACC Method 39-11.01 standardises its determination in wheat flour by NIR reflectance, calibrated against a Kjeldahl or combustion reference [11]. Ash has no such signature, because minerals do not absorb in the near-infrared. AACC Method 08-21.01 is explicit about this: flour ash correlates very highly with the cellulosic content of bran, and NIR ash calibrations are likely associated with the cellulosic components of bran fragments in the flour rather than with the ash itself [1].

The published results reflect that difference. Dowell et al., working with 100 hard red winter and 98 hard red spring wheat samples on four NIR instruments, predicted protein and moisture with R² above 0.97, an accuracy they describe as suitable for process control, whereas flour ash reached an R² of at most 0.56 in any instrument and sample-set combination [3]. Osborne gives the typical NIR measurement time as 15 to 90 s and notes that the analysis can be carried out on-line [10]. Recent work with visible/near-infrared hyperspectral imaging and neural-network or PLS models reported ash prediction accuracies from 92.27 % to 99.55 % on two Iranian flat-bread flours [5]. An NIR ash value is thus an indirect estimate of bran particles, valid for the wheat and the mill it was calibrated on. Screening the incoming wheat is a separate calibration: Miralbés predicted protein and moisture of whole wheat by NIR transmittance with r² of 0.99 for both and judged the models adequate for screening at the receiving stage of mills and elevators [12].

Monitoring flour streams in practice

ISO 12099 gives the guidelines for applying NIR to cereals and milled cereal products, from calibration through validation to ongoing monitoring against the reference method [13]. For a mill that wants ash and protein values from its streams while they flow, three points follow:

  • Calibrate per stream population and validate against incineration. Because the NIR ash model tracks bran particles rather than minerals [1], a model built on straight-grade flour cannot be assumed to hold on a tail-end reduction stream or a different wheat mix; each population is validated against ISO 2171 or AACC 08-01.01 results [6][8].
  • Treat NIR ash as a screening signal and protein as a control value. In the Dowell et al. data set the protein accuracies were of process-control grade and the ash accuracies were not [3]; the NIR ash trend detects a drift in a stream, and a change of flour grade is confirmed by incineration.
  • Keep the reference under control. An NIR value cannot be more accurate than the furnace result it was trained on [10]; a fixed incineration protocol such as ICC 104/1 [7] and a stated moisture basis keep the calibration anchored.
