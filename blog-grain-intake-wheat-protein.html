NIR for Wheat Protein and Moisture at Grain Intake

Why protein and moisture decide how a wheat delivery is segregated, which reference methods and standards near-infrared measurements rest on, what published whole-grain studies report including their limits, and how an NIR step is run at grain intake.

Protein, moisture and the value of a delivery

At the intake of a flour mill, elevator or feed plant in the food and feed industry, each wheat delivery has to be assessed while the truck is still on site, because once it is tipped into a bin it is blended with other lots. Protein is the primary factor in determining the end use of wheat, and grain from cereal crops is segregated on the basis of crude protein and moisture content using near-infrared (NIR) instruments that work in either reflectance or transmittance [1]. Moisture enters the decision in two ways: it is measured and reported for the lot in its own right, and protein results are expressed on a fixed moisture basis or on dry matter, so an error in moisture carries into the protein figure [2][3].

Values used for segregation have to be comparable between instruments and sites. Networks of intake analyzers have been built for exactly this purpose: the Danish NIR transmission network determines protein, moisture and starch in barley and wheat, moisture in rye and oil in rapeseed, with field instruments standardised against a sub-master [4].

The reference methods behind the numbers

NIR is a secondary method: its predictions can be no better than the reference values it was calibrated on. For moisture, ISO 712-1:2024 specifies the routine reference method for cereals and cereal products, covering wheat in the form of grains, milled grains, semolina or flour; it replaced ISO 712:2009 and does not apply to maize or pulses [5]. For protein, nitrogen is determined either by the Kjeldahl method of ISO 20483 or by Dumas combustion according to ISO 16634-2, and crude protein is calculated from the nitrogen content with a conversion factor [6][7]. Neither method distinguishes protein nitrogen from non-protein nitrogen [6][7].

A calibration therefore inherits the conventions of its reference data: the nitrogen method, the conversion factor and the moisture basis. In the AOAC collaborative study of whole-grain NIR for wheat protein, for example, combustion analysis (AOAC 990.03) was the reference procedure and reference protein values were corrected to a 12 % moisture basis [2]. When intake results are compared with a buyer's or a reference laboratory's figures, these conventions have to match before any difference is attributed to the NIR instrument.

Whole-grain NIR in published studies

Measuring whole kernels is what makes NIR practical at intake, because the sample needs no grinding. In the collaborative study by Delwiche et al., four types of commercial NIR instruments were standardised on 22 hard red winter wheat samples and then applied to 12 unknown samples ranging from 9 to 16 % protein; root mean square differences from the reference were 0.22 to 0.26 % protein depending on instrument type [2]. Repeatability and reproducibility were equivalent to the values reported for the combustion reference method for wheat, the bias-corrected errors were about twice the estimated lower limit set by sample inhomogeneity, and the method was adopted by AOAC INTERNATIONAL as First Action Method 997.06 [2]. In Europe, EN 15948:2020 defines a routine NIR method for moisture and protein in whole kernels of wheat and barley, for wheat over minimum ranges of 8 to 22 % moisture and 7 to 20 % protein on dry matter, and it sets out separate duties for the equipment supplier and for the user [3].

Both measurement modes are used on whole grain. Williams and Sobering compared commercial transmittance and reflectance instruments on several grains and seeds and found the two approaches comparable in accuracy and reproducibility, while showing that the wavelength range has to be optimised for whole-grain analysis [8]. For moisture, Osborne reported a single NIR reflectance calibration for white flour, ground wheat and whole wheat with a predictive accuracy of 0.22 % against oven drying on 68 samples [9].

The limits are real. Whole kernels scatter light in a more complex way than a ground sample: Manley et al. found no evidence that scatter affects the reflectance and transmittance spectra of whole wheat in the simple multiplicative way that common scatter corrections assume [10]. The calibration population matters as much as the optics. Cozzolino et al. obtained a standard error of prediction of 0.35 % for crude protein from whole-grain reflectance spectra of 100 breeding lines, judged the models suitable for screening, and concluded that more genotypes and crop years were needed to improve accuracy [1]. Williams and Sobering introduced the idea of monitoring whole-grain calibrations against ground-grain calibrations [8], which is where a sample mill and a ground-sample method enter the routine.

A practical workflow at the intake

The standards and studies cited above point to three practices that keep an intake measurement defensible:

  • The sample has to come from the whole load, not only from the top. ISO 24333 sets requirements for static or dynamic, manual or mechanical sampling of cereals for the assessment of quality and condition [11]. An NIR result describes only the portion placed in the sample cell.
  • Instruments have to be kept matched and checked. In the Danish network a standardisation with grain samples had to be carried out at least once a year for optimal field performance, after which systematic instrument-to-instrument differences were virtually removed [4]. ISO 12099 gives the guidelines for applying NIR to cereals and milled cereal products, including moisture and protein [12]; sending a share of intake samples to the reference laboratory on a regular schedule allows bias or drift to be detected before it affects segregation (see also calibration transfer).
  • Sample temperature has to stay within the calibration's range. If deliveries arrive at temperatures not represented in the calibration set, either let samples equilibrate or use a calibration that includes temperature variation. Abe et al. showed that such temperature-compensated calibrations depend on the regression algorithm and that simple multiple linear regression is not fully adequate for them [13].

Calibrations should also be extended over time. New crop years, varieties and growing regions change the population the model has to cover, and the whole-grain study above concluded that adding genotypes and crop years was the route to better accuracy [1].
