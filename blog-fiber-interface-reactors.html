Optical Fiber Interface Designs for Corrosive Chemical Reactors

How the sampling geometry, the fibre-optic link, the window material, the wetted alloy and the seal decide what a near-infrared spectrometer actually sees inside a corrosive or high-temperature reactor — with the materials data and spectroscopy literature behind each choice.

Why the interface decides what the spectrometer sees

Near-infrared spectroscopy earns its place in the chemical and pharmaceutical industry largely because the measurement point can be separated from the instrument: Reich lists the separation of sample position and spectrometer by fibre-optic probes among the technique's main advantages [1]. Inside a reactor the price of that convenience is a wetted interface that must tolerate the medium, its temperature and its pressure while keeping a stable optical path. The calibration sees only the light that returns through that interface, which is why the standard texts treat sample presentation as part of the instrument [2].

Transmission, transflectance or ATR

Three geometries cover most liquid-phase reactor work. A transmission probe passes the light once across a gap between two windows; a transflectance probe returns it with a mirror so that it crosses the gap twice and the probe becomes a single insertion with the fibres at one end; attenuated total reflectance (ATR) guides the light inside a high-index crystal and senses only the sample layer in contact with it. Fahrenfort described the ATR principle: radiation arriving from the dense medium above the critical angle is totally reflected except where the sample absorbs, giving a high-contrast spectrum that resembles a transmission spectrum [3]. ASTM E573 collects the theory, influencing parameters and practical guidelines for internal reflection measurements [4].

Because ATR senses only the layer at the crystal surface, it suits strongly absorbing, viscous or particle-laden media where a transmission gap would foul, whereas the weaker overtone and combination bands of the NIR region are usually measured in transmission or transflectance with a gap matched to the absorbance of the bands used [2][4]. Crystal material matters as much as geometry: Kemsley et al. built a sapphire ATR crystal into a stainless-steel fibre-optic probe because the usual ATR crystals lack food-contact approval, choosing sapphire for being hard, inert and non-toxic [5].

The fibre link: hydroxyl and hydrogen

Silica fibre carries the light between analyzer and probe, and its own absorption is part of the measurement. Humbach et al. report that bound hydroxyl (SiOH) in silica produces a fundamental band at about 2.7 µm and that its overtone and combination bands, including the one at 1.38 µm, govern the OH-related transmission of a fibre [6]. Fibre grade (low-OH for the long-wavelength region) and fibre length therefore set how much of the usable range survives the round trip, since the fibre's OH bands overlap the water and combination bands used by many calibrations [6].

Hydrogen is the second mechanism. Stone's review covers overtone absorption by molecular hydrogen, formation of OH groups, the common sources of hydrogen in fibre and cable materials, and the countermeasures against hydrogen intrusion [7]. In a hydrogenation reactor or a hydrogen-rich plant atmosphere, hermetically coated fibre and the cable materials around it are part of the optical design [7].

Windows and wetted alloys

Sapphire (single-crystal Al2O3) is the usual window for reactor probes; its mechanical, chemical and optical properties are compiled by Dobrovinskaya et al. [8]. Gryvnak and Burch found that the absorption coefficient of sapphire increases at most wavelengths between 0.56 and 6 µm as the temperature rises, and that the refractive index below 4 µm also rises with temperature [9]. Window transmission and optical path are thus not strictly temperature-independent, one argument for collecting the background spectrum with the probe at process temperature [9].

Shaft and window seat must be matched to the chemistry. Haynes' data for HASTELLOY C-276, a nickel-chromium-molybdenum alloy, show a large improvement over 316L and 254SMO stainless steels in hydrochloric acid above about 5 %, resistance to oxidising and non-oxidising acids, and high resistance to chloride-induced pitting, crevice attack and stress-corrosion cracking [10]. The same datasheet notes that weld metal corrodes faster than wrought material and that field tests are encouraged before industrial use [10]. The limits are real: in Haynes' saturated wet-chlorine test, C-276 coupons in the liquid phase at 80 °C suffered severe weld-metal corrosion and intergranular attack, whereas C-22 showed no localised attack [11]. A probe is welded and often carries a different alloy at the window seat, so the wetted-materials list belongs in the application review, not in a catalogue default [10][11].

Sealing, pressure and conformity

The window-to-metal joint is where optical and mechanical design meet: elastomer seals allow window exchange but have temperature and chemical limits, and a soldered or brazed joint extends the range at the cost of thermal-stress management. Ockenfels et al. bonded a sapphire window by active soldering to a metal flange through two C-shaped rings of different metals that mitigate thermal stress, and operated a cell with two such viewports between 20 and 450 °C at pressures up to 330 bar without measurable leakage [12]. In the European Union, pressure equipment and assemblies with a maximum allowable pressure above 0.5 bar fall under Directive 2014/68/EU, so whether an inserted probe counts as a pressure accessory of the vessel, and what rating and documentation it needs, is a conformity question rather than a brochure claim [13]. Three practice points follow:

  • Choose the geometry from the medium. Clear or moderately scattering liquids: transmission or transflectance; fouling, viscous or strongly absorbing media: ATR, accepting that only the surface layer is sensed [3][4].
  • Specify the fibre, not just the probe. Low-OH fibre, the shortest run the layout allows, and hermetically coated fibre where hydrogen is present [6][7].
  • Reference and validate on the installed probe. Background spectra at process temperature and a calibration built through the actual window, fibre and gap, because window absorption and fibre loss are part of the measured spectrum [6][9].

How USTECH approaches this

The ProLine2550 process FT-NIR analyzer is specified for inline use on reactors, pipelines and blenders with liquids, slurries and simple solid flows, and the ProLine17EC places an InGaAs-array NIR sensor behind an O-ring-sealed sapphire window in a 316 stainless-steel housing. Interface geometry, wetted alloy and seal are chosen from the medium, temperature and pressure of the process; the calibration is then built in caliX on spectra collected through the installed interface and connected to the control system through ProChem.
