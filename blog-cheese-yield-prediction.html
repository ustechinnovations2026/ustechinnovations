Evaluating Cheese Yield Prediction Models

How cheese yield is predicted from milk composition, where the classic yield formulas fall short, what near- and mid-infrared spectroscopy add at milk intake and during coagulation and syneresis in the vat, and how a yield model should be built and validated — with the peer-reviewed literature behind each point.

Why cheese yield matters

Cheese is a very important trade item in the dairy industry; Fox et al. put it at about 35 % of total milk usage [1]. Yield is expressed as kilograms of cheese per 100 kg of milk at standardised fat and protein levels, and plants track yield efficiency, the actual yield as a percentage of the yield predicted from milk composition [1]. The factors that move it are well catalogued — milk composition and quality, standardisation, heat treatment, gel firmness at cutting, cutting and stirring regime, curd particle size and vat design — and they act by influencing the composition of the milk gel and how much moisture is expelled and how much fat is lost from the curd particles [1].

Predicting yield from milk composition

Predictive yield formulas descend from one based only on casein and fat, published in 1895; the Van Slyke formula is the best-known of the line, and later refinements added cheese moisture and salt, whey solids and paracasein in place of casein [2]. Emmons and Modler tested the General, Barbano and Van Slyke formulas on 22 vats of Cheddar and found that the unadjusted formulas did not predict yield accurately: the mean predicted yield was 99.17 % of actual, and the measured cheese components summed to less than 100 % [2]. Adjusting for the observed sum of components brought the mean to 99.99 % of actual, so adjusted formulas are recommended for target yields and yield efficiency; notably, total milk protein then gave the same predicted yields as casein, so a casein determination may not always be necessary [2].

The inputs are the fat and protein of the vat milk, and those values are only as good as the reference methods behind them: the gravimetric fat reference is ISO 23318 | IDF 249, which replaced ISO 1211 [3], and ISO 8968-1 | IDF 20-1 determines nitrogen by the Kjeldahl principle and calculates crude protein from it [4].

What infrared spectroscopy adds at milk intake

The reference methods are slow relative to the pace of cheese making, so spectroscopic calibrations against them supply the day-to-day numbers. Laporte and Paquin determined fat, crude protein, true protein and casein in cow's milk by near-infrared transmission with PLS calibrations on 96 samples; independent validation gave standard errors of prediction of 0.07 % for fat, 0.06 % for crude protein and casein and 0.05 % for true protein, and homogenisation was found to affect the fat determination [5].

A more direct route is to calibrate the spectrum against yield itself. Ferragina et al. made 1,264 model cheeses from individual-cow milk under a fixed laboratory protocol and related mid-infrared (FTIR) spectra of the unprocessed milk to the measured yield traits; cross-validation gave coefficients of determination of 0.95 for total-solids cheese yield and 0.83 for fresh cheese yield, but only 0.65 for water retained in the curd and 0.41 for fat recovery [6]. The spectrum carries the compositional information that drives solids yield; water retention and fat loss are decided in the vat and are not visible in the milk spectrum. That is the part of the problem in-vat monitoring addresses.

Monitoring coagulation and syneresis in the vat

O'Callaghan et al. reviewed the on-line techniques for monitoring coagulum formation — hot-wire, vibrating and optical probes — and concluded from factory trials spanning seasonal milk variation that on-line monitoring can improve the consistency of the coagulum at cutting [7]. The optical approach relies on the rise in light scattering as casein micelles aggregate. Castillo et al. monitored goat's milk coagulation with a diffuse-reflectance sensor at 880 nm; the reflectance parameter Tmax correlated with the Berridge clotting time (R² = 0.9913), and a cutting-time equation based on Tmax predicted cutting time with a standard error of 84.5 s (R² = 0.9785) across varied pH, temperature and enzyme concentration [8]. Lyndgaard et al. extended this to full NIR spectra (1,000–2,500 nm) recorded in-line, compressed by principal component analysis and fitted with a kinetic model separating κ-casein proteolysis, micelle aggregation and network formation; it fitted 12 batches with R² above 0.99 and allowed real-time estimation for automatic cutting-time determination [9].

The same signal carries information after cutting. Fagan et al. showed by response surface methodology that fat retention is a function of cutting time and temperature, that curd yield also depends on calcium chloride addition, and that there is an optimum gel firmness at cutting, to be maximised while avoiding rapid coarsening of the gel and microsyneresis [10]. In a follow-up, equations combining milk composition with backscatter parameters predicted fat losses with a standard error of prediction of ±2.65 g/100 g (R² = 0.93), curd yield with ±0.95 % (R² = 0.90) and curd moisture with ±1.43 % (R² = 0.94) [11]. These were pilot-vat studies over deliberately varied conditions; they show that the signal contains the information, not what a production vat will achieve.

Building a yield model that holds up

A cheese-yield model is really two models: a composition-based prediction of the yield the milk should give, and an in-vat model of how much of that potential is realised. Three practices keep both defensible:

  • Define the yield you are predicting. Ferragina et al. distinguish fresh yield, total-solids yield and water retained in the curd, plus the recovery of fat, protein and total solids [6]; a model calibrated on one cannot be read as another.
  • Anchor the calibration to the reference methods. Fat and protein values should come from ISO 23318 and ISO 8968-1 [3][4], and NIR calibration and validation on milk products follows ISO 21543 | IDF 201 [12].
  • Validate across seasons. Cutting time and the syneresis response depend on pH, temperature, calcium and enzyme level [8][10], and the factory review stresses seasonal variation [7]; re-validate before trusting a model on the next season's milk.
