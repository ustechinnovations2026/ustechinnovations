Inline vs. At-Line vs. Off-Line Analysis: Which Approach Is Right for Your Production Line?

How the FDA PAT guidance defines at-line, on-line and in-line measurement, what each location trades away in delay, sampling error and validation effort, and how to decide which arrangement a production line actually needs — based on the regulatory texts, the Theory of Sampling and the NIR process literature.

Why the location of the measurement matters

Where a measurement is made relative to the process decides what the result can be used for: a laboratory value that arrives after the batch has moved on documents quality, a value available while the material is still in the line can control it. Kowalski put it plainly: sampling errors and the time delays of sample transport and analysis made it nearly impossible to control complex chemical processes with the required degree of success [1]. The FDA guidance on process analytical technology (PAT) defines PAT as a system for designing, analysing and controlling manufacturing through timely measurements, made during processing, of critical quality and performance attributes, and restates that quality cannot be tested into products but should be built in by design [2]; ICH Q8(R2) carries the same definition into its glossary [3]. For a food, feed or chemical plant the question is which combination of measurement locations supports the decisions the process has to make.

Off-line, at-line, on-line, in-line: the definitions

The vocabulary is older than the PAT initiative, but the FDA guidance gives the definitions most often cited today. Under the heading “Process Analyzers” it states that measurements can be [2]:

“at-line: Measurement where the sample is removed, isolated from, and analyzed in close proximity to the process stream. on-line: Measurement where the sample is diverted from the manufacturing process, and may be returned to the process stream. in-line: Measurement where the sample is not removed from the process stream and can be invasive or noninvasive.” [2]

Off-line analysis is the case the guidance does not need to define: the sample is removed and carried to a laboratory, the traditional arrangement Kowalski describes [1]. Two points are easily overlooked. On-line differs from in-line in whether the material leaves the main stream, for example through a bypass loop; suppliers commonly say “inline” for both. And in-line measurement may be invasive, with a probe in the material, or non-invasive, through a window [2]; the EMA guideline on near-infrared spectroscopy accordingly treats the sampling interface or probe as an essential component of an NIR method that must be described and controlled [4].

What each location trades away

Off-line analysis offers controlled conditions and reference methods, and it remains the basis for calibration and for release decisions under a conventional control strategy [3][4]. Its costs are the delay and, less visibly, the sample itself. The Theory of Sampling holds that only correct, unbiased mass reduction yields a representative sample and that the total sampling error is by far the dominating contribution to all analytical endeavours, often more than 100 times larger than the total analytical error [5]. A grab sample scooped from a conveyor carries that error into the result however precisely it is analysed, and non-representative sampling cannot be repaired afterwards by statistics or chemometrics [6].

At-line analysis removes the transport and the laboratory queue, not the sampling: the instrument stands beside the line and an operator brings the sample to it. For solid raw materials this is where benchtop NIR fits; ISO 12099 gives guidelines for applying NIR spectrometry to feeding stuffs, cereals and milled cereal products, from calibration against reference methods to validation and monitoring in routine use [7]. Wherever the instrument stands, the performance of a quantitative NIR procedure depends on the performance of the reference method; poor reference data limit what the model can achieve [4].

In-line and on-line measurement remove both the transport and the manual sample. Huang et al. list the resulting advantages in food processing: the instrument works in the line under realistic conditions, failures are detected early and the process is monitored permanently; NIR suits this role because it needs no sample preparation and measures several constituents at once [8]. Porep et al. describe the hardware, from fibre-optic and direct-contact probes to tube-integrated probes measuring through windows and measurements on conveyor belts, and note that in numerous studies an on-line application was claimed but not actually performed [9]. The trade-off is that the sampling interface now defines the sample. Esbensen and Sivalingam argue that the interface must both define an appropriate sample volume and suit the analytical technique, and that most current process sampling interfaces do not comply with the Theory of Sampling, which puts representativity at risk for heterogeneous materials [10]. The EMA guideline turns this into expectations for on-line NIR: optimise the location of the sampling device, estimate the effective sample size, keep the window covered with sample and control fouling [4].

Choosing the arrangement for a given line

The FDA frames the choice as a design task: identify the critical attributes, design a measurement system that allows real-time or near-real-time monitoring of them, whether on-, in- or at-line, and design the controls that act on the measurements [2]; ICH Q8(R2) calls the resulting planned set of controls the control strategy [3]. Three questions settle most cases:

  • What decision does the result feed, and how fast? A drying end-point or a moisture set-point calls for in-line or on-line data a control system can act on; the FDA notes that such measurements need not be absolute values, since relative differences within and between lots already support feed-forward and feed-back control [2]. Lot acceptance and disputes remain reference-method decisions.
  • Can a representative sample reach the analyzer? Establish how heterogeneous the material is and where a correct increment can be taken before choosing a probe or a bypass; a well-designed bypass or at-line arrangement can be more representative than a badly placed in-line window [5][10].
  • How will the method be validated and maintained? Moving a laboratory method on-, in- or at-line is not in itself PAT, and method-validation guidance still applies [2]; the EMA expects the reference method, sampling interface, probe position and sampling plans to be specified and the procedure re-appraised regularly [4].

Most plants end up with a combination. The FDA lists shorter cycle times through on-, in- and/or at-line measurements, and real-time release, among the gains expected from PAT, the latter justified by showing that real-time quality assurance is at least equivalent to laboratory testing of collected samples [2]; ICH Q8(R2) adds that real-time release testing can replace end-product testing but not the GMP review steps needed to release the batch [3]. The laboratory does not disappear; its role shifts to reference analysis, calibration maintenance and investigation.
