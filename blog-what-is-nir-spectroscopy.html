What Is NIR Spectroscopy?

A plain-language introduction to near-infrared spectroscopy — the spectral region, its history from Herschel to Norris, the overtone and combination bands it records, why every quantitative result rests on a chemometric calibration, and where the method is strong and where it is weak — based on pharmacopoeial texts, standards and review literature.

What near-infrared light is

Near-infrared (NIR) radiation is the part of the electromagnetic spectrum between visible light and the mid-infrared. Its edges are drawn slightly differently depending on the source: the pharmacopoeial chapter USP <856> defines the NIR range as 780 to 2,500 nm, about 12,821 to 4,000 cm−1 [1], while the optics-and-photonics standard ISO 20473 assigns the near-infrared band to 0.78–3 µm [2]. Analytical reviews quote 750–2,500 nm or 800–2,500 nm [3][4]. None of these sources starts the region as low as 700 nm, a limit that is sometimes quoted informally [1][2][3][4]. USP <1856> further divides the range into a short-wavelength “Herschel” region of about 780–1,100 nm and the longer-wavelength region from 1,100 to 2,500 nm [5].

From Herschel to Norris

Near-infrared was the first kind of non-visible radiation to be discovered [4]. In 1800 William Herschel dispersed sunlight with a prism and placed thermometers beyond the visible red end of the spectrum; they still warmed, and he described the invisible rays as having “a high power of heating bodies, but with none of illuminating objects” [6]. Analytical use followed much later. Pasquini traces it to efforts to develop instrumental methods for agriculture and names Karl Norris, who began working with NIR while searching for new methods to determine moisture in agricultural products, as the most prominent figure in the history of the technique [3].

What an NIR spectrum records

Molecules absorb mid-infrared radiation at the frequencies of their fundamental vibrations. The NIR region, by contrast, is dominated by overtones and combinations of those fundamentals [1][3]. Such transitions are only allowed because bond vibrations are anharmonic, and the O–H, C–H, N–H and S–H bonds, with their high anharmonicity and high bond energy, give the strongest NIR features [3]. Beć and Huck describe the X–H bands as the most strongly articulated part of NIR spectra and point out that their positions and intensities are governed by anharmonic effects [4]. Band assignments for the main functional groups are collected in interpretive atlases such as the one by Workman and Weyer [7].

Overtone and combination bands are weak: USP <856> notes that NIR molar absorptivities are much lower than in the mid-infrared and that the radiation penetrates several millimetres into a material [1]. This weakness is an advantage in practice. Samples can be measured as they are, often by diffuse reflectance without pre-treatment [3], and the measurement probes the bulk rather than only the surface [4]. How the light is dispersed or modulated to record a spectrum is a separate topic, covered in What Is FT-NIR? and in the comparison of NIR and FT-NIR architectures.

Why NIR needs chemometrics

NIR bands are broad and overlap heavily, and the observed spectrum is usually the sum of many contributions [4]. A single wavelength therefore seldom carries enough information for a quantitative model, and Pasquini calls NIR the analytical technique that most applies chemometrics [3]. The consequence is that NIR is an indirect, secondary method: it is heavily dependent on good and acceptable reference methods, and robust models may require hundreds or even thousands of samples analysed by the reference method [3]. Beć and Huck warn that analytical NIR is then easily reduced to a “black-box tool” that quantifies without explaining [4], and Pasquini’s later review discusses the drawbacks and misuses of the technique and of its supporting mathematical tools [8]. For feed, cereals and milled cereal products, ISO 12099 sets out guidelines for calibration, validation, outlier handling and monitoring of instrument stability [9].

Where NIR is used, and where it reaches its limits

In the food industry, NIR has been applied to intact solids, free-flowing solids, pastes and fluids, both for quantitative analysis of constituents and for qualitative tasks such as authenticity control, including on-line measurements on conveyor belts, in pipes and in fermentation [10]. ISO 12099 covers NIR determination of constituents such as moisture, fat, protein, starch and crude fibre in feed and cereals [9]. In pharmaceutical technology, NIR is used for solid, liquid and biotechnological forms, in development, in production for process monitoring and in quality-control laboratories, for both identification and quantification [11]. The method also has clear limits:

  • Indirect method. Every quantitative result inherits the quality of the reference analyses and of the calibration set; a model is valid only for samples like those it was built on [3][9].
  • Trace analytes. NIR is not very sensitive; Pasquini gives a typical detection limit of about 0.1 % (m/m) and notes that most quantitative applications target major constituents [3].
  • Minerals and salts. Species without the O–H, C–H, N–H or S–H bonds that dominate NIR spectra can only be determined indirectly, for example sodium chloride in concentrated aqueous solution through its effect on the water bands [3].
