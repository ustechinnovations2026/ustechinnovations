Blend Uniformity Verification in Active Pharmaceutical Blending

Why blend uniformity is a regulated in-process attribute, what thief sampling can and cannot tell you, and what the published literature shows about in-line near-infrared monitoring of powder blending within the FDA PAT framework.

Why blend uniformity is a regulated attribute

In solid dosage manufacturing the dose in each tablet depends on how well the active ingredient has been blended with the excipients. In the chemical and pharmaceutical industry this is a regulatory matter: US good manufacturing practice requires in-process controls that include, where appropriate, “adequacy of mixing to assure uniformity and homogeneity” (21 CFR 211.110(a)(3)) [1]. The finished product is then tested under the harmonised pharmacopoeial chapter on uniformity of dosage units, in which content uniformity is assessed on 10 units, and if necessary on 30, through an acceptance value that must not exceed L1 = 15.0 unless otherwise specified [2].

The FDA's 2004 PAT guidance observed that most pharmaceutical processes were run to time-defined end points such as “blend for 10 minutes”, which do not account for physical differences between raw-material lots, and set out a framework in which critical quality attributes are measured during processing, because quality cannot be tested into products but has to be built in [3]. ICH Q8(R2) places such measurements within a control strategy and defines real-time release testing as evaluating and ensuring quality from process data, typically a combination of measured material attributes and process controls [4].

The limits of thief sampling

The conventional check is to stop the blender, insert a sampling thief at predefined locations and assay the samples. Muzzio et al. showed that thief samples can carry considerable errors that lead to misleading conclusions, that about 30 samples characterise a well-mixed blend but hundreds are needed for a poorly mixed one, and that the state of the blend in the blender does not necessarily represent the mixture after subsequent handling [5]. After the FDA withdrew its draft guidance on stratified in-process sampling, an ISPE working group published proposed modifications to it: criteria for when triplicate blend samples should be analysed, variance-component analysis to find the root cause of non-uniformity, and acceptance criteria based on ASTM E2810 and E2709 [6]. ASTM E2810 applies the methodology of E2709 to the uniformity-of-dosage-units test, computing from a sample's mean and standard deviation a lower bound, at a chosen confidence level, on the probability of passing that test [7]. The underlying limitation remains: a handful of samples, taken after the blender has stopped and assayed later in the laboratory.

What in-line NIR monitoring of blending shows

Sekulic et al. fitted a diffuse-reflectance fibre-optic probe at the axis of rotation of a V-blender; the spectra of a model blend containing 10 % sodium benzoate as the active, with microcrystalline cellulose, lactose and magnesium stearate, converged to a point of constant variance, and the blend was shown to be homogeneous long before a typical blending period was complete [8]. El-Hagrasy et al. monitored a salicylic acid–lactose blend through optical ports at six positions on a V-blender, with thief sampling and UV assay as reference; multiple sampling points proved essential for an accurate estimate of the end point, and repeated runs of identical blends often reached homogeneity at different times, which is the strongest argument for monitoring every blend rather than relying on a fixed time [9].

Two families of data treatment have developed from this work. The quantitative approach calibrates the spectra against composition: Berntsson et al. inserted a fibre-optic diffuse-reflection probe into the powder in the blender and used a simplified multivariate calibration, built from samples prepared and analysed off-line, to determine the content of the mixture during processing and so follow both content and homogeneity in real time [10]. The qualitative approach tracks the variability between successive spectra and declares an end point when it falls to a preset minimum: Puchert et al. combined principal component analysis with distances between successive scores, moving-block standard deviations of those distances and Hotelling's T² statistic, and built a design space for blend homogeneity from 10 bin-blender batches verified independently by NIR chemical imaging [11].

From spectra to an end-point decision

Whatever the approach, the spectra are first corrected for the physical effects of a moving powder bed: Rinnan et al. review the scatter corrections (MSC, SNV, normalisation) and Savitzky-Golay derivatives used to remove baseline and multiplicative effects before chemometric modelling [12]. Over-aggressive pre-processing can remove part of the composition signal on which the end-point statistic depends. Three points decide whether an in-line blend monitor is useful in practice:

  • Where and how often the blend is viewed. A single probe sees one region of the powder bed, and El-Hagrasy et al. found multiple sampling points essential [9]; probe position and spectra per revolution are design decisions, not defaults.
  • How the end point is defined. A plateau in the moving-block standard deviation of successive spectra must be linked to a demonstrated state of homogeneity, as Puchert et al. did with independent chemical imaging [11]; the plateau alone is not evidence of uniformity.
  • How the method is validated. ASTM E2898 gives a risk-based framework for validating at-line, on-line and in-line PAT methods, in which the ICH validation parameters still apply but are demonstrated in ways suited to on-line measurement [13]; the link to the dosage-unit test is made through ASTM E2810 [7] and the ISPE recommendations [6].

In-line NIR does not replace the pharmacopoeial test; it moves the evidence of adequate mixing from a few thief samples to a continuous record of the whole blending operation, which is what the PAT guidance means by measurement during processing [3].
