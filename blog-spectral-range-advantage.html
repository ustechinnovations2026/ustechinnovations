Choosing the Near-Infrared Spectral Region

What the short-wave, first-overtone and combination regions of the near-infrared spectrum each carry, which detector technologies reach them, and the trade-off between band specificity and penetration depth — based on the spectroscopic and application literature.

Why the wavelength region matters

Analysers used in the food and feed industry and in process plants differ not only in optical design but in which part of the near-infrared they record. Pasquini defines the NIR region as 750–2,500 nm and explains that its absorptions are overtones and combinations of the fundamental vibrations of O–H, C–H, N–H and S–H bonds, whose fundamentals lie at 3,000–4,000 nm [1]. These bands are much weaker than the fundamentals, and Beć and Huck note that absorptivity decreases steadily toward the higher overtones and higher-order combinations [1][2]. That single fact drives the main trade-off of this guide: where bands are strong and specific, light penetrates only a short distance; where light penetrates deeply, bands are weak and heavily overlapped.

The three regions and what they carry

Short-wave NIR (below about 1,100 nm) contains the high-order overtones: Gonzaga and Pasquini built a low-cost spectrophotometer for 850–1,050 nm and could assign spectral regions to C–H stretching third overtones [3]. Bands here are so weak that path length must be increased to see them: in Pasquini's chloroform example, the 850–1,150 nm region shows almost no structure at a 1 mm path but five distinct bands at 5 cm [1].

The first-overtone region (roughly 1,100–1,800 nm) carries the first overtones of the X–H stretches and is where standard InGaAs instruments collect most of their information; Lukacs et al., for example, found major regression-vector peaks at about 1,400–1,600 nm, assigned to N–H first overtones, when quantifying nitrogen-rich adulterants in protein powders [4].

The combination region, given by Pasquini as 1,600–2,500 nm, holds the combination bands, which are less attenuated than the overtones, and is also where resonances can split one band into two [1]. Lukacs et al. saw N–H combination features above about 2,000 nm in the same study [4]. Beć and Huck stress that the whole NIR spectrum is built from a very large number of overlapping, anharmonic contributions, so even here individual bands are rarely isolated [2].

Penetration depth: the other side of band strength

Weak absorption is what lets NIR light probe a bulk sample rather than only its surface [2]. Measured penetration depths fall with increasing wavelength. Padalkar and Pleshko found about 5 mm in cartilage at 7,000–9,000 cm−1 (about 1,110–1,430 nm), about 3 mm at 5,100–7,000 cm−1, and only 1–2 mm at 4,000–5,100 cm−1, the combination region [5]. In avocado fruit, Wedding et al. measured transmission penetration of roughly 12 mm near 834–885 nm but less than 3 mm between about 1,348 and 1,862 nm [6]. Pasquini makes the same point for liquids: cell path length must shrink as one moves from the higher overtones to the combination region around 2,200 nm [1].

This is why short-wave NIR is used for intact produce. Kawano et al. predicted sugar in intact peaches in interactance mode using second-derivative terms at 906, 878, 870 and 889 nm [7]. The limits are equally documented: Guerra et al. used two pocket-sized instruments working at 740–1,070 nm on 331 milk samples and obtained an adequate fat model (external-validation R² 0.93) but poor models for lactose, urea, somatic cells and freezing point, concluding that the region holds too little information for those traits [8].

Detectors decide which region an instrument sees

Pasquini lists silicon, PbS and InGaAs as the most frequently used NIR detector materials [1]. Beć et al. report that Si photodiodes keep reasonable sensitivity from the visible to about 1,100 nm and suit compact, low-cost instruments, that InGaAs performs best at around 1,000–1,600 nm with fast response and low dark current, and that extended InGaAs has lower sensitivity and may need cooling [9]. Instruments can combine detectors: the grating benchtop in the Lukacs et al. study used Si for 400–1,098 nm and PbS for 1,100–2,498 nm [4]. Crocombe's review documents how visible and short-wave NIR engines have made consumer-priced devices possible [10]. Long-wavelength coverage is therefore a detector choice, not something reserved for one optical architecture [4][9].

Choosing a region in practice

No region is best for every sample. The literature points to three questions:

  • Where is the information for the analyte? Constituents with weak or indirect short-wave signatures may need the overtone or combination region [8]; check published band assignments and pilot spectra before fixing the range [2][4].
  • How thick and heterogeneous is the sample? Intact or coarse samples favour shorter wavelengths, which see deeper into the material; clear liquids in short cells can use the combination region [1][5][6].
  • What does the sampling interface allow? Lukacs et al. excluded wavelengths above 2,200 nm because fibre-optic probes introduce high noise there [4]; detector sensitivity and cooling needs also change across the range [9].
