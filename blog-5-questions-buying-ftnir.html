5 Questions to Ask Before Buying an Inline NIR Analyzer

Five questions that decide whether an inline near-infrared analyzer will deliver trustworthy results for years, from measurability and sample interface to calibration ownership, data integrity and lifecycle validation, each grounded in the published standards and literature.

Why the questions matter more than the data sheet

An inline analyzer is bought for the measurement it delivers over its life, not for its specification sheet. The FDA's process analytical technology (PAT) guidance frames such instruments as part of a system for controlling manufacturing through timely measurements of critical quality attributes [1]. Sector guidelines exist for this chain: ISO 12099 for feed, cereals and milled cereal products [2] and ISO 23291 | IDF 248 for in-line and on-line infrared measurement in dairy processing [3], both relevant to plants in the food and feed industry. Spectrometer architecture is covered in NIR vs FT-NIR and measurement location in inline, at-line or off-line.

1. Can NIR measure the parameter, and how well against the reference method?

NIR results are predictions from a multivariate calibration against a reference method. ASTM E1655 sets out practices for such calibrations in the near-infrared region (roughly 780 to 2500 nm) and criteria for validating their performance [4], and ISO 12099 defines the performance statistics to report: bias, root mean square error of prediction, standard error of prediction and slope, computed on samples that were not used to build the model [2]. Ask for these, from an independent validation set of the same matrix, not for calibration fit statistics.

Those figures have to be read against the reference laboratory. Faber and Kowalski showed that a prediction error estimated against measured reference values is an apparent error, systematically larger than the true error, because the random error of the reference result cannot be predicted by any model [5]. DiFoggio, conversely, lists the belief that NIR can never outperform its reference method among recurrent misconceptions [6]. Esbensen et al. criticise blind reliance on single indices such as the RPD [7]. The EMA guideline draws the practical conclusion: reference uncertainty should be low relative to the intended performance [8].

2. Does the sample interface see a representative sample?

An inline probe or window analyses only the material in front of it. The Theory of Sampling describes the errors that arise when heterogeneous materials are sampled and holds that only correct, unbiased mass reduction yields a representative sample [9]. For on-line NIR, the EMA guideline expects the presentation of the material to the probe to be optimised and validated, including the location of the sampling device, an estimate of the effective sample size, assurance that the window is covered with sample and controls against fouling [8]. A vendor should be able to state, for the actual pipe, chute or vessel, what volume each spectrum represents and how fouling is detected.

3. Who develops, validates and maintains the calibration?

The EMA guideline recommends updating models as new batches become available, expects changes in process, composition or site to trigger an update, and requires every change to be validated and documented [8]. Before purchase, clarify who owns the spectra, reference data and model, and who performs updates. If several units will run one model, ask how transfer is handled. Workman's review concludes that identical results from several instruments with one calibration still elude practitioners [10]. The EMA describes two routes — a bias, slope or vector correction where instruments are similar, or recalibration and revalidation on the new unit — each backed by a comparability protocol [8]; see calibration transfer.

4. How does the result reach the control system, and can it be trusted?

Protocols and signal paths are covered in PLC and SCADA integration; the buying question is what accompanies each value. Where records fall under FDA requirements, 21 CFR Part 11 requires validated systems, limited access and secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trails for entries that create, modify or delete electronic records [11]. Equally important is a validity flag with every prediction. ASTM D6122 states that a measurement cannot be validated unless its applicability is established, and that a result from an outlier spectrum is invalid [12]. A bare number leaves the control system unable to tell a process change from a spectrum the model never saw.

5. How will it be qualified, and what will it cost to keep running?

ASTM D6122 was written for petroleum product and fuel analyzers, but its structure is general: predictions are compared with the primary test method on samples not used in the model, and procedures verify that instrument, model and analyzer system remain stable [12]. ASTM E1655 refers this ongoing validation to D6122 [4]. The EMA lists lamp and sampling-device replacement, relocation and software upgrades among changes that require documented revalidation and comparability [8]. These are recurring costs the purchase price does not show.

  • Budget the reference analyses. Initial validation and ongoing checks both need reference results on line samples [2][12], and their precision bounds what the analyzer can demonstrate [5][8].
  • Ask for the change procedure in writing. Lamp replacement, software upgrades and model updates should come with a defined revalidation and comparability step [8].
