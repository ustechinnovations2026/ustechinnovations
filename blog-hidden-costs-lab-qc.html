The Hidden Costs of Lab-Based Quality Control in Food and Feed Manufacturing

Why the price of a laboratory result is larger than the invoice for reagents and analyst time: how result latency, sampling error, over-formulation and the chemistry of the reference methods each carry a cost, what the process-analytical and sampling literature says about them, and where inline or at-line NIR changes the picture.

Where the hidden costs come from

In the food and feed industry the direct cost of a quality-control laboratory is easy to read off a budget: instruments, reagents, consumables and analyst time. The indirect cost is paid elsewhere — in product made while a result was pending, in the margin added to a formulation so that a late result would still pass, and in decisions based on a sample that did not represent the lot. The process analytical technology (PAT) literature was written for pharmaceuticals, but its central argument applies to any process: measure during processing, not only after it [1][2].

"Quality cannot be tested into products; it should be built-in or should be by design." — FDA PAT guidance [1]

Result latency: measuring after the fact

The US FDA guidance defines PAT as controlling manufacturing through timely measurements, made during processing, of critical quality attributes, and distinguishes at-line, on-line and in-line measurement by whether the sample is removed from, diverted from or left in the process stream [1]. The gains it anticipates are the hidden costs of the laboratory model in reverse: shorter cycle times, fewer rejects, less scrap and re-processing, better use of energy and materials [1]. ICH Q8(R2) adds that real-time release testing based on process data can replace end-product testing, though not the GMP batch review [2].

A laboratory result arrives late because of the chemistry of the reference methods. Crude protein by Kjeldahl (ISO 5983-1) requires digestion in sulphuric acid with potassium sulphate and a copper catalyst at 420 °C, then steam distillation of the ammonia into boric acid and titration [3][4]. Crude fat (ISO 6492) is determined by solvent extraction, and feeds containing milk products, gluten, yeast, soya or potato proteins or heat-treated ingredients need a hydrolysis step before extraction can begin [5]. Every step consumes reagents that must be bought, handled and disposed of, and lengthens the interval between sampling and knowing the answer. The collaborative study of the copper-catalyst method reported within-laboratory relative standard deviations for crude protein of 0.40 % to 2.38 % [4]: that is the precision bought with the delay, and the floor for any faster method calibrated against it.

Sampling error: the cost nobody budgets

A laboratory analyses a few grams; the decision applies to tonnes. The Theory of Sampling developed by Pierre Gy treats the selection of the test portion as an error source in its own right, with rules for how and how much to sample, and warns that there is no constant sampling bias that could simply be calibrated away [6]. Petersen, Minkkinen and Esbensen put the consequence plainly: the total sampling error dominates analytical results, often more than 100 times larger than the total analytical error [7]. Esbensen and Paasch-Mortensen apply the same theory to reactors, vessels and process streams and call representative sampling the missing link in PAT [8]. For a grab sample from a conveyor, a bin or a tanker, the practical point is that a precise analysis of a poorly selected sample is precise about the wrong thing, and the cost appears as accept, reject or adjust decisions made on a number that did not describe the lot.

Giveaway and over-formulation: paying for the safety margin

When a result arrives hours after the product is made, the only way to be sure a batch will pass is to aim well inside the limit. Hunter and Kartha worked out the statistics for filling lines: a process target is set some distance from its specification limit on the safe side, and the further it is set the fewer items are rejected, but an offsetting give-away cost limits how far the target can profitably move, with the optimum depending on the process variability [9]. Reduce the variability, or measure it sooner, and the target can move closer to the limit.

The same logic applies to composition: a feed formulated to a protein minimum, a flour to a moisture maximum or a dairy powder to a fat specification is "filled" with a margin of protein, drying energy or fat, exactly as a can is filled with a margin of product. The regulatory example is the EU average-fill system: Directive 76/211/EEC requires that the actual contents of a batch are not less, on average, than the nominal quantity, and that the proportion of packages exceeding a tolerable negative error is small enough to pass the tests in Annex II [10]. A producer who cannot see the batch distribution in time buys compliance with overfill.

What process analysis changes, and what it does not

Near-infrared spectroscopy is the usual choice for on- and in-line composition measurement in food because it needs no sample preparation, can be mounted in the production line, measures several constituents at once and monitors continuously rather than at sampling intervals [11]. Porep, Kammerer and Carle describe traditional methods as labour-intensive, time-consuming and expensive and NIR as a low running-cost alternative, but caution that many studies claiming on-line application were in fact laboratory work [12]. For compound feed, Pérez-Marín et al. developed calibrations on unground samples that they judged reliable enough for quality control at the feed mill, with R² 0.97 and SECV 5.5 g/kg for crude protein [13].

None of this abolishes the laboratory: an NIR calibration is built from and validated against the reference methods, so the Kjeldahl and extraction results remain the anchor [3][5][13]. What changes is where the cost is paid:

  • Measure where the decision is made. An at-line analyser answers a batch-release question, an in-line analyser a control question [1]; choose the location by the decision, not by the instrument.
  • Treat the sample interface as part of the method. An in-line probe sees a small volume of a moving stream; the Theory of Sampling applies to it as much as to a scoop, so probe location and averaging time should be validated against representative reference samples [7][8].
  • Re-set the target only after the variance is known. Moving a formulation target towards its limit is justified by the measured process distribution [9], not by the presence of a faster instrument.
