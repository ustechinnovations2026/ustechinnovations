Rapid Adulteration Detection in Raw Milk Receiving

Why the receiving dock is the critical control point for economically motivated adulteration of raw milk, what peer-reviewed studies show near- and mid-infrared spectroscopy can detect, and how a screening step fits into a dairy intake line.

Why raw milk is adulterated

Milk is graded and priced on compositional parameters such as fat and protein, which has made it a long-standing target for economically motivated adulteration. Reviews of the field list dilution with water, addition of whey or other foreign proteins, vegetable or animal fats, and nitrogen-rich compounds such as urea and melamine among the most frequently reported adulterants [1][2].

Nitrogen-rich adulterants exploit a limitation of the reference protein method. The Kjeldahl procedure (ISO 8968-1 | IDF 20-1) determines total nitrogen and calculates crude protein from it; the standard itself notes that inaccurate crude protein results are obtained when non-milk sources of nitrogen are present [3]. In the 2008 incident in China, melamine was added to diluted raw milk at collection stations to raise its apparent protein content; around 300,000 infants and young children were affected, six died, and contaminated products were withdrawn worldwide [4].

Why the receiving dock matters

Once a tanker is discharged into a bulk silo, its contents are blended with every other delivery: an adulterated load can no longer be separated, and the consequences scale from one tanker to the whole silo. The reference methods for adulterants — chromatography, mass spectrometry, immunoassays, electrophoresis — are laboratory techniques and are not designed to return a verdict while a tanker waits at the bay [2][5]. Pre-discharge screening therefore calls for a method that is rapid, needs little or no sample preparation and can be operated by intake staff.

What infrared spectroscopy can detect

Near-infrared (NIR) and mid-infrared (MIR) spectroscopy combined with chemometric models have been studied for this purpose for almost two decades. Kasemsumran et al. showed that NIR spectra (1,100–2,500 nm) analysed with discriminant PLS and SIMCA classification, and with PLS regression, can detect and estimate water and whey added to cow milk [6]. Santos et al. used MIR microspectroscopy with SIMCA and PLS regression to separate control milk from milk spiked with whey, urea, synthetic milk, synthetic urine and hydrogen peroxide [7]. For melamine, Balabin and Smirnov reported a detection limit of about 0.76 ppm using NIR/MIR with multivariate regression across liquid milk, infant formula and milk powder [8], and Jawaid et al. reported a detection limit of 2.5 ppm in liquid and powdered milk by ATR-FTIR, with an assay time of one to two minutes [9]. Guidance on applying NIR to milk and milk products is given in ISO 21543 | IDF 201 [10].

These figures come from laboratory studies on deliberately spiked samples. The detection limit, false-alarm rate and calibration robustness obtainable at a specific intake line depend on the milk population, the adulterants included in the model and the sampling set-up, and must be established with a site-specific validation set before any screening model is used to reject a delivery.

A screening workflow at the intake line

Where spectroscopic screening is deployed at receiving, the pattern reported in the literature is consistent: a sample drawn from the tanker's sampling port is brought to a controlled temperature and measured in transmission through a short-path liquid cell; a classification model (SIMCA or PLS-DA) flags spectra that fall outside the population of authentic milk; and a quantitative PLS model estimates the parameters it has been calibrated for [6][7]. Three practices keep such a step defensible:

  • Screen, then confirm. A spectroscopic flag identifies a suspect delivery; confirmation of a specific adulterant should rest on a reference method — for example freezing-point depression by thermistor cryoscopy (ISO 5764 | IDF 108) for extraneous water [11], or chromatographic methods for melamine [2].
  • Re-validate the model. The population of authentic milk shifts with season, breed and feeding, so the classification boundary needs periodic re-validation against fresh reference samples.
  • Keep a record per delivery. A time-stamped result for every tanker supports traceability. Note that the US FSMA rule on intentional adulteration [12] is a food-defence framework aimed at acts intended to cause wide-scale public health harm; it is not a regulation of economically motivated adulteration, and intake screening should not be presented as compliance with it.
