Starch Damage and Gluten Quality in Durum Wheat Semolina

What starch damage and gluten quantity and strength mean for durum wheat semolina and pasta, how the reference methods determine them, what peer-reviewed work shows near-infrared spectroscopy can and cannot predict, and where an at-line measurement fits — with sources.

Why these parameters matter for pasta

Durum wheat semolina is the raw material of conventional dry pasta, and semolina that is high in both protein and gluten content is recognised as the ideal raw material for it [1]. The Codex standard for durum wheat semolina and durum wheat flour defines the products and sets a maximum moisture of 14.5 % m/m, but leaves the technological quality factors — gluten, starch damage, particle size — to the agreement between miller and pasta maker [2]. For mills in the food and feed industry that supply pasta plants, those factors are what the specification is written around.

Gluten quantity and gluten strength

Gluten forms the protein network that holds pasta together during extrusion, drying and cooking. Cubadda et al. reconstituted semolinas from two cultivars of good and poor gluten quality at identical protein contents and found that optimum cooking time is governed by gluten quality; raising gluten content and applying high-temperature drying improved cooking quality in the weak-gluten series but had no significant effect in the strong-gluten series [3]. Quantity and strength are therefore separate properties, and both are specified.

Both are measured in one procedure. ISO 21415-2 specifies the determination of wet gluten content and gluten index by mechanical means for flours of common and durum wheat, and it can also be applied to ground grain when the particle size distribution meets the standard's requirements; it is related to AACC Method 38-12.02 [4][5]. A dough is washed in the instrument to isolate the wet gluten, which is weighed and then centrifuged through a sieve: wet gluten content describes how much gluten the sample yields, and the gluten index describes how strong it is from the share retained on the sieve [4][5]. In winter durum wheat, Vida et al. found the gluten index to depend little on the environment and to be determined to the greatest extent by genotype, which makes it chiefly a variety and wheat-mix property [6].

Starch damage: what it does and how it is measured

Roller milling mechanically damages part of the starch granules, and damaged granules absorb more water. Dexter et al. milled Canadian durum composites to three levels of starch damage and found starch damage to be the predominant factor influencing Farinograph water absorption, development time and stability [7]. In pasta making the effect is on hydration: Bresciani et al. list damaged starch with protein, ash, fibre and particle size among the semolina properties that govern hydration, note that semolina low in ash and damaged starch gives an amber-yellow dried product with fewer brown specks and less heat damage, and describe the miller's trade-off — coarser semolina lowers starch damage, while pasta producers report homogeneous hydration with particles between 250 and 450 µm and white spots with coarser material [1].

Two families of reference method exist. AACC Method 76-31.01 is a spectrophotometric method based on enzymatic digestion of the damaged starch [8]. Amperometric methods are faster: AACC Method 76-33.01 specifies the amperometric determination with the SDmatic instrument [9], and ISO 17715 specifies an amperometric method for flour from common wheat (Triticum aestivum) [10]. The scope of ISO 17715 does not name durum semolina, so a mill applying an amperometric method to semolina should document the correlation with the enzymatic method on its own products. The methods report in different units, and results are not interchangeable without a conversion established on the same samples.

What NIR can and cannot predict

Near-infrared calibrations for starch damage have been published. Osborne and Douglas calibrated NIR reflectance on 148 UK commercial flour samples, obtaining a correlation coefficient of 0.906 and a residual standard deviation of 3.5 Farrand units over a range of 6–47 Farrand units, rising to 4.2 Farrand units on 68 further samples; the selected wavelengths corresponded to free and hydrogen-bonded O–H vibrations in starch [11]. That is a bread-wheat flour calibration with an error that is a sizeable fraction of the range, and it does not establish an accuracy for durum semolina, whose coarser particle size alters diffuse reflectance.

For durum wheat itself, Sissons et al. developed NIR calibrations in a breeding programme and validated them on large independent sets: protein and moisture were predicted with high accuracy, hectolitre weight, semolina yield, semolina colour and hardness usefully, but dough properties could not be adequately predicted from whole-grain spectra [12]. Protein, which NIR measures well, is not the same as gluten strength, which it measured poorly in that study. ISO 12099 sets out how any such calibration is to be validated against the reference method for cereals and milled cereal products [13].

Where an at-line measurement fits

  • Use NIR for composition. Moisture and protein of semolina are the parameters with the strongest published NIR record [12]; calibrations are validated per product against the reference methods as ISO 12099 describes [13].
  • Measure starch damage with a reference-type method. Given the limited published NIR accuracy [11], starch damage that steers roll settings is determined with an enzymatic or amperometric method [8][9][10], with the correlation between them documented on semolina.
  • Keep gluten testing for gluten. Wet gluten and gluten index come from ISO 21415-2 or AACC 38-12.02 [4][5]; because the gluten index is largely a varietal property [6], it is controlled mainly at wheat intake and blending.

How USTECH approaches this

For starch damage USTECH offers the StarchQC, a benchtop amperometric analyzer (not an NIR instrument) of the type covered by the amperometric methods above [9][10]. For moisture and protein, the MasterLine17EC at-line NIR analyzer is used with grains and milled products, and the caliX suite provides the PLS calibration and validation tools that ISO 12099 describes [13]. Which parameters reach the accuracy a semolina specification requires is established in a feasibility study with the mill's own samples and reference analyses, not assumed from published figures.
