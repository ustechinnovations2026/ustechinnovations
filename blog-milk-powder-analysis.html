Moisture and Protein Analysis in Milk Powder by NIR Spectroscopy

How moisture, protein, fat and lactose in skimmed and whole milk powder are determined by the ISO/IDF reference methods, what peer-reviewed studies report for near-infrared spectroscopy on these parameters, and how an at-line or inline NIR measurement is calibrated and validated against them.

Why composition is checked on every lot of powder

Milk powders are traded on their composition. The Codex Standard for Milk Powders and Cream Powder (CXS 207-1999) sets a maximum water content of 5 % m/m for cream, whole, partly skimmed and skimmed milk powder alike, a minimum milk protein content of 34 % m/m in the milk solids-not-fat, a maximum milkfat of 1.5 % m/m for skimmed milk powder and a milkfat range of 26 % to below 42 % m/m for whole milk powder [1]. Two details of the same standard matter for any analyser: the water figure excludes the water of crystallisation of lactose, and the milk solids-not-fat figure includes it [1]. A dairy plant therefore needs a moisture result that corresponds to the Codex definition, and a protein result that it can relate to the solids-not-fat basis, on every lot that leaves the dryer.

The reference methods

Moisture is determined by the oven-drying reference method ISO 5537 | IDF 26, whose second edition (2023) cancels and replaces the 2004 edition and applies to all types of dried milk and dried milk products [2]. Protein is derived from total nitrogen by the Kjeldahl procedure ISO 8968-1 | IDF 20-1, which also specifies the crude-protein calculation [3]. Fat in dried milk products is now covered by the gravimetric method ISO 23318 | IDF 249:2022, whose scope lists whole, partially skimmed and skimmed milk powder, whey powder and milk-based infant formula powder, and which replaced the separate dried-milk standard ISO 1736:2008 [4]. Lactose is determined by high-performance liquid chromatography according to ISO 22662 | IDF 198:2024, which includes dried milk in its scope [5]. Each of these methods involves sample preparation, chemical reagents or chromatography, and skilled laboratory work; their results arrive after the powder that produced the sample has long left the dryer.

What near-infrared spectroscopy sees in a powder

Water analysis was the first successful application of NIR spectroscopy; two strong, broad absorption bands near 1,440 and 1,930 nm appear in almost every NIR spectrum, and hydrogen bonding and sample temperature both affect the reliability of water determinations [6]. Protein, fat and lactose contribute overlapping overtone and combination bands, so a multivariate calibration is needed to separate them. Holroyd's review of NIR on milk and milk products describes the technique as commonly applied to gross compositional parameters such as fat, protein and moisture, stresses the importance of appropriate sample preparation, and summarises the wavelengths relevant to milk products [7].

The quantitative record goes back decades. Baer et al. correlated proximate data from 82 non-fat dry milk samples with filter-instrument NIR reflectance and reported a correlation coefficient of 0.971 with a standard error of prediction of 0.274 for moisture, 0.961 and 0.099 for fat, 0.887 and 0.594 for lactose and 0.905 and 0.438 for Kjeldahl protein [8]. Wu et al. used the short-wave region (800–1,050 nm) with PLS and least-squares support vector machines, evaluated standard normal variate pre-treatment, proposed a wavelength assignment for milk powder and obtained coefficients of determination for prediction of 0.981, 0.984 and 0.982 for fat, protein and carbohydrate [9]. More recently, Peng et al. combined a miniaturised NIR spectrometer with random-forest regression on yak milk powder and reported correlation coefficients of 0.9846, 0.9642 and 0.9915 for protein, fat and moisture [10]. The consistent picture is that moisture, fat and protein are well predicted, lactose somewhat less so, and that every figure is specific to the powder population and reference data behind it.

At-line or inline: calibration and validation

NIR is a secondary method: a calibration relates spectra to reference values obtained by the methods above, and its accuracy cannot exceed theirs. ISO 21543 | IDF 201:2020 gives the guidelines for applying NIR spectrometry to milk and milk products in liquid, semi-solid or solid form by transmittance, diffuse reflectance or transflectance, and covers the selection of calibration samples, reference analyses, calibration and outlier handling [11]. For the process itself, Holroyd et al. reviewed a number of in-line and on-line NIR installations for milk powder and concluded that Fourier-transform NIR systems can reliably measure the moisture, fat and protein composition of milk powder in-line [12]. Three practices keep either configuration defensible:

  • Define moisture the way the specification does. The Codex water limit excludes lactose water of crystallisation [1], and the ISO 5537 oven method is the reference against which an NIR moisture model is calibrated [2]; a model trained on a different water definition will read against a different limit.
  • Control temperature and presentation. Sample temperature and hydrogen bonding shift the water bands [6], and sample preparation is singled out in the dairy NIR literature as a determinant of accuracy [7]; the at-line cup or the inline window should see powder under the same conditions as the calibration set.
  • Validate per product, then monitor. Skimmed, whole and fat-filled powders are different populations; ISO 21543 describes validation and outlier handling for this reason [11], and periodic comparison against reference results should continue after the model is in use.

How USTECH approaches this

For at-line checks beside the dryer or in the plant laboratory, the USTECH MasterLine17EC benchtop NIR analyzer measures powders in a sample dish; for continuous measurement at a transfer point, the ProLine2550 inline FT-NIR analyzer is offered in a configuration for powders and granules.
