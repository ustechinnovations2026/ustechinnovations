Standardizing Reference Background Measurement Intervals in Process Spectroscopy

Why a process near-infrared spectrometer needs a reference (background) measurement, what makes its response drift, how instrument performance tests are defined and how drift is detected in routine use — based on the published literature and the relevant ASTM practices.

Why the reference measurement matters

A near-infrared analyzer in a chemical or pharmaceutical process does not measure absorbance directly. It records a raw intensity spectrum that contains the emission profile of the source, the transmission of the optics and the response of the detector, and reflectance or transmittance is obtained by ratioing that spectrum against a reference measurement. A multivariate calibration built on such spectra assumes that the instrument part of the signal stays the same from day to day. Workman notes that obtaining identical results over time from the same calibration remains difficult, and lists instrument reproducibility and repeatability and sample presentation among the factors involved [1]. Wise and Roginski describe calibration model maintenance as necessary precisely because the sample matrix or the instrument response changes over time [2].

Reference materials: what is measured as “background”

For diffuse reflectance the usual reference is a highly reflecting, nearly Lambertian white material. Pressed polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) powder was characterised at the US National Bureau of Standards as a diffuse reflectance standard, with absolute reflectance reported from 200 to 2500 nm, and the same work showed that its reflectance depends on preparation density and thickness and that contaminants can cause fluorescence [3]. Sintered PTFE materials such as Spectralon are widely used for the same purpose; Stiegman et al. found an intrinsic hydrocarbon impurity in commercial Spectralon that caused reflectance degradation under UV exposure and could be removed by vacuum bake-out [4]. The reference is therefore itself an optical component whose cleanliness and condition need to be controlled. A wavelength reference is a separate matter: NIST SRM 2036 is a rare-earth oxide glass in contact with sintered PTFE, certified for the positions of seven bands between 975 nm and 1946 nm, and is intended for verifying the wavelength scale of NIR spectrometers in diffuse or trans-reflectance mode [5].

Sources of drift

Drift has instrument-side and process-side causes, and a reference measurement corrects only part of them:

  • Instrument response. Workman’s chapter on instrument performance treats short-, medium- and long-term drift, wavelength accuracy and precision, photometric accuracy, linearity and noise as separate quantities to be tested [6]. A fresh reference ratio compensates changes in source intensity and detector response that are common to sample and reference, but not a shift of the wavelength axis, which needs a wavelength standard [5][6].
  • Temperature. Temperature changes the shape of vibrational spectra in a nonlinear way; Wülfert et al. showed on short-wave NIR spectra of ethanol/water/2-propanol mixtures that this degrades the predictive ability of local PLS models, while global models that include temperature variation are more robust at some cost in sensitivity [7]. Hageman et al. review the methods for building temperature-robust calibrations [8]. This is a sample effect and is not removed by re-referencing.
  • Process interface. Deposits on the probe window are part of the optical path in inline installations. In an in-line study of fluidized bed drying, Bogomolov et al. found that probe fouling had only a minor effect on moisture prediction accuracy, with the probe periodically cleaned and recalibrated without interrupting the process [9]; the size of the effect is application-specific and should be checked rather than assumed.

Instrument performance tests

ASTM E1866 gives the basic procedures for designing spectrophotometer performance tests in the UV, visible, NIR and mid-IR, including univariate Level Zero and Level One tests and multivariate Level A and Level B tests for rapid routine checks; it also applies to on-line process spectrophotometers [10]. Such tests flag a malfunction or a change in operation but do not diagnose or quantify it, and they are not meant for comparing instruments from different manufacturers [10]. ASTM E1944 defines Level Zero and Level One tests for laboratory FT-NIR spectrometers, letting an analyst compare an instrument’s performance on a given day with its earlier performance using a compatible check sample; it is explicitly not intended for dedicated process FT-NIR analyzers [11]. Wavelength-scale checks rely on certified standards such as SRM 2036 [5], and the photometric and noise tests and their standards are described in the NIR handbook literature [6].

How often to re-reference and how drift is detected

None of the sources above prescribes a universal re-referencing interval; the frequency follows from how quickly the instrument and its environment change and from what the performance tests show. ASTM D6122-25, which covers online, at-line and laboratory infrared and Raman analyzers using multivariate models, requires that instrument performance is verified, that each spectrum is checked against the calibration space by outlier detection, and that results agree with the primary test method within user-defined limits [12]. It notes that instrument performance tests can show whether the outlier methods respond to changes in instrument response [12]. In routine use this gives three complementary signals: a trend in performance-test results, a rise in spectral outlier statistics, and a growing difference from laboratory reference values. Wise and Roginski propose a roadmap for deciding from such evidence when maintenance is needed and whether the cause lies in the instrument or in the samples [2]; if the instrument response itself has changed, standardisation and calibration transfer methods apply [1].

How USTECH approaches this

The USTECH ProLine2550 process FT-NIR analyzer combines integrated temperature control with automatic calibration against an internal wavelength reference and is supplied with a reference calibration tile; the ProLine17EC monitors source health and optical window integrity; and the MasterLine17EC at-line analyzer uses a Spectralon reference standard. Which reference schedule and which checks a given installation needs is established in a feasibility study with your own samples and process conditions.
