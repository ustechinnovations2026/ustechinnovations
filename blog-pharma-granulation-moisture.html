Moisture Monitoring in Fluid Bed Granulation

Why residual moisture is the critical quality attribute of a fluid-bed granulation, how loss on drying and Karl Fischer titration differ as reference methods, what peer-reviewed in-line NIR studies have shown for drying end-point determination, and why sampling, probe fouling and temperature decide whether the method holds up in production.

Why moisture is the critical attribute

Fluid-bed granulation is a widely used wet-granulation step in the pharmaceutical industry: a binder liquid is sprayed onto fluidising powder, the wetted particles collide and form permanent aggregates, and the wet granules are then dried in the same vessel [1]. The European NIRS guideline names granule drying and control of moisture content among the critical process parameters and quality attributes to be considered for tablets [2]; in ICH Q8(R2) terms, residual moisture is a critical quality attribute that should stay within an appropriate limit, range or distribution [3]. ICH Q8(R2) and the FDA's PAT framework both define process analytical technology as controlling manufacturing through timely measurements, made during processing, of such attributes [3][4]; in-line NIR moisture measurement in the granulator bowl is among the most studied implementations of that idea [1].

What the reference methods measure

A granulate's moisture is reported against one of two pharmacopoeial procedures, and they do not measure the same thing. Loss on drying, USP general chapter <731>, determines the amount of volatile matter of any kind driven off under the specified conditions, so residual solvents count as well as water; where water is the only volatile constituent the chapter itself points to Water Determination <921> [5]. Chapter <921> provides titrimetric (Karl Fischer), azeotropic and gravimetric methods, with titrimetric Method Ia as the default unless the monograph specifies otherwise [6]. The distinction matters for NIR, which responds to the O–H overtone and combination bands rather than to total volatiles: a model trained on loss-on-drying values of a granulate wetted with an alcohol-containing binder inherits whatever solvent is still present in the reference samples. The EMA guideline requires calibration and validation samples to be analysed by the reference method and keeps that method in the authorised specification [2].

What in-line NIR studies have shown

Frake et al. tracked granule moisture and particle-size change in-line by NIR during fluid-bed granulation and used the real-time trace to adjust process conditions and identify the end-point [7]. Rantanen et al., evaluating a four-wavelength in-line sensor, found that a few wavelengths around the water band give reliable, high-speed detection, provided that the binder bands (near 1700 nm and 2200 nm for the binders studied, plus an N–H band near 1500 nm for gelatin) and the baseline rise caused by growing particle size are considered when the wavelengths are chosen [8]. Findlay, Peck and Morris monitored moisture and particle size through a window into the bed; both correlated well with off-line measurements, and with predefined peak-moisture, final-moisture and final-size values the system could decide when to stop binder addition and when drying was complete [9]. Peinado et al. took the method into commercial production: a PLS model on in-line spectra was validated by adapting ICH Q2(R1) to a method built exclusively from in-line samples, was reviewed and approved by FDA and EMA, and is used as the primary in-line control of the drying end-point to a limit of not more than 1.2 % w/w [10]. Burggraeve et al. review these and the other in-line analysers applied to fluid-bed granulation [1].

Sampling, probe fouling and temperature

Three effects separate a promising development study from a robust production method. The first is sampling. Green et al., working at 65-, 300- and 600-L drying scale, showed that process heterogeneity plays a major role in the apparent prediction accuracy of in-line NIR moisture methods, that the sampling configuration with the smallest apparent error is not necessarily the best for process control, and that the configuration also determines how many samples a calibration set needs [11]. The reference value comes from a thief sample while the probe sees a different portion of the bed, so part of any disagreement is sampling error rather than spectroscopic error [11].

The second is the sampling interface: wet granules adhere to surfaces, and a window coated with product reports the coating, not the bed. The EMA guideline lists, among the risk-assessment items for an in-line procedure, optimisation of the sampling-device location, assurance that the window is covered with sample, and controls to ensure that there is no fouling on the device [2]. The third is temperature, which the guideline names among the variables whose effect on the spectral response must be shown to be insignificant or controlled with data [2]. Wülfert, Kok and Smilde showed on short-wave NIR spectra of water-containing mixtures that temperature changes the spectra non-linearly, that PLS models calibrated without temperature variation lose predictive ability when it appears, and that global models which include temperature variation in the calibration set predict considerably better, at the cost of sensitivity [12].

A workflow consistent with the guidance

The EMA guideline describes an NIRS procedure as a defined scope, a method (sampling interface, probe position, sampling plan) and a model, managed under lifecycle change control [2], and USP <856> is the compendial chapter governing qualification, validation and verification of NIR systems [13]. Three practical consequences follow:

  • Calibrate along the whole drying trajectory. Reference samples drawn at the wet start, during drying and at the dry end should span the bed temperatures and particle sizes the probe will see [8][11][12].
  • Define the end-point as a rule, not a reading. A prediction below the limit, confirmed by the reference method, is the pattern accepted in a regulated application [10]; the reference method stays in the specification [2].
  • Treat the probe as part of the method. Probe position, window coverage and fouling control belong in the method description and the risk assessment [2][11].

How USTECH approaches this

For the granulator bowl USTECH offers the ProLine17EC and ProLine17ECS inline NIR analyzers, rugged IP65 units with a sapphire sample window and an InGaAs array detector. Spectral pre-processing, PLS regression and validation against the loss-on-drying or Karl Fischer reference are done in the caliX chemometrics software, and ProChem passes the predicted moisture to the PLC or SCADA system so that the end-point rule can be acted on in the control layer. Whether an in-line method reaches the accuracy a given granulation step requires, and how probe position and fouling control must be set for that product, is established in a feasibility study with your own granulate samples [2][11][13].
