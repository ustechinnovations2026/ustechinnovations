Real-Time Fat Analysis in Ground Beef Processing

Why fat content is the controlling specification for ground beef in US and EU labelling rules, what the reference methods for fat, moisture and protein involve, and what the peer-reviewed on-line NIR studies at grinder outlets and on conveyor belts report about accuracy, grind size and batch averaging.

Why fat content is the controlling specification

Ground beef is bought, blended and sold on its lean-to-fat ratio, so fat content is regulated, declared and priced at the same time. In the United States, 9 CFR 319.15 defines chopped beef, ground beef and hamburger as products that shall not contain more than 30 percent fat and shall not contain added water, phosphates, binders or extenders [1]. The labelling rule 9 CFR 317.362(f) allows a lean percentage statement on ground meat only if the fat percentage appears contiguous to it in lettering of the same colour, size and type [2]. In the European Union, Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 (Annex VI, Part B) fixes maximum fat contents for the designation “minced meat”, checked on a daily average: 7 % for lean minced meat, 20 % for minced pure beef, 30 % for minced meat containing pigmeat and 25 % for other species, and requires the expression “percentage of fat content under …” on the label [3]. For a processor in the food industry the task is two-sided: never exceed the declared fat level, and never give away lean meat by running further below it than necessary.

The reference methods and why they are slow

The reference methods for meat composition are classical wet-chemistry procedures. ISO 1443 determines total fat by acid hydrolysis of the sample to release bound fat, followed by solvent extraction [4]. ISO 1442:2023 determines moisture by direct drying, or by distillation for products with many volatile substances besides water [5]. ISO 937:2023 determines nitrogen by the Kjeldahl principle, from which crude protein is calculated [6]. Each takes hours of digestion, extraction or drying, so the result refers to a batch that has already left the mixer or grinder. Prieto et al. found NIR well suited to predicting the chemical composition of meat but weaker for technological and sensory traits, and attribute the weaker results to the heterogeneity of the samples and their preparation, the low precision of reference methods and the subjectivity of taste panels [7].

What the on-line studies report

Continuous measurement of ground beef at the point of production has a peer-reviewed record going back three decades, much of it from the Norwegian group around Isaksson, Tøgersen and Hildrum. Isaksson et al. mounted a diffuse-reflectance NIR instrument directly at a meat grinder outlet and, for samples covering 6.2–21.7 % fat, 59.6–72.9 % moisture and 18.1–20.7 % protein, obtained cross-validation errors (RMSECV) of 0.73–1.50 % for fat, 0.75–1.33 % for moisture and 0.23–0.32 % for protein depending on the grinder plate; the smallest hole diameters gave the lowest errors, and the results were judged acceptable for 4, 8 and 13 mm plates [8]. Tøgersen et al. took the same approach to industrial-scale batches of beef and pork ground through a 13 mm plate (7–26 % fat, 58–75 % water, 15–21 % protein) and reported prediction errors of 0.82–1.49 % for fat, 0.94–1.33 % for water and 0.35–0.70 % for protein; the method was adopted for routine use at a Norwegian meat plant in place of manual standardisation [9].

Later studies addressed the awkward cases. For semi-frozen beef, Tøgersen et al. used a non-contact filter instrument at the grinder outlet, with filters chosen to be as insensitive as possible to water crystallisation, and estimated each batch from the average of its readings; on 55 batches of 400–800 kg the RMSECV was 0.48–1.11 % for fat, 0.43–0.97 % for moisture and 0.41–0.47 % for protein [10]. On a conveyor belt, Hildrum et al. monitored 60 batches of coarsely ground beef with a diode-array instrument under industrial conditions; after removing the signal of the belt itself, cross-validated prediction errors were 1.6–2.4 % for fat and water and 0.5–0.8 % for protein, again best at the smallest grinding size [11]. Anderson and Walker measured a continuous moulded stream of ground beef with a visible/NIR diode-array spectrometer and obtained a standard error of prediction of 2.15–2.28 % fat on single validation measurements but 0.70–1.05 % fat when predictions were pooled over whole blocks [12]. Wold et al. report the same effect for an NIR imaging scanner on beef trimmings: single trimmings were predicted with an RMSEP of 8.7 %, streams forming batches of 10–24 kg with 1.33 %, and simulations indicated about 0.6 % for 100 kg batches [13].

Designing the measurement point

The literature gives three practical rules for placing an on-line analyzer in a ground beef line:

  • Report batch averages, not single spectra. Ground meat is heterogeneous at the scale of a measurement spot, and the on-line studies reached usable accuracy by averaging readings over a batch or a block [10][12][13]. The control decision — adding lean or fat trimmings to the mixer — belongs on the batch average.
  • Measure after the finest grind available. Meat ground through the smallest plate holes gave the lowest errors both at the grinder outlet and on the belt [8][11]; the coarser the product, the more material must be averaged for the same error.
  • Keep the sample population under control. Semi-frozen meat changes the spectrum through ice formation and needs its own wavelength selection or calibration [10]; a belt background has to be removed from conveyor measurements [11]; and since the model inherits the error of the reference method, the ISO fat, moisture and nitrogen procedures [4][5][6] should be run under documented conditions for reference samples.

Turning the running fat and moisture estimate into a lean/fat standardisation loop means adjusting the proportions of lean and fat raw material before the batch is discharged, which is how the Norwegian plant in the 1999 study used it [9]. The declared value on the label remains a matter for the reference method and the regulation; the on-line NIR result complements that analysis rather than replacing it [1][3].
