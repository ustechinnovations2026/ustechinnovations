Hydroxyl Value Monitoring in Polymerization Processes

What the hydroxyl value and the acid value say about a step-growth polyesterification, how the titrimetric reference methods are defined, why the reaction is visible in the near-infrared, what the kinetics mean for end-point detection, and what the published in-reactor NIR work shows — with the standards and literature behind each point.

Two numbers that describe a polycondensation

Polyester resins and the polyester polyols used as polyurethane precursors are made by step-growth polycondensation of polycarboxylic acids or anhydrides with polyols, with water distilled out of the reactor as it forms [1]. In the chemical industry the batch is followed by two titration numbers. The acid value, defined for polyester resins and binders in ISO 2114, counts the unreacted carboxyl groups [2]; the hydroxyl value, defined for binders in ISO 4629-1, counts the free hydroxyl groups, which in a polyester are the chain-end hydroxyls of the diol [3]. For a polyol destined for polyurethane, the hydroxyl number is also the figure against which the isocyanate stoichiometry is set, which is why ASTM D4274 states that the hydroxyl content of polyols must be known to formulate polyurethane systems [4].

What the values mean and how they are measured

Both values are expressed in milligrams of potassium hydroxide per gram of sample: for the acid value, the KOH needed to neutralise the acidic groups; for the hydroxyl value, the KOH equivalent to the hydroxyl content of one gram [2][3]. In a linear step-growth polymer every chain carries two end groups, so the sum of the acid and hydroxyl values counts chain ends and is inversely related to the number-average molecular weight; end-group analysis of this kind is one of the classical ways of measuring the molecular weight of condensation polymers [5]. The two numbers therefore track the same conversion from opposite sides, and their ratio reflects the acid–diol excess chosen at the start of the batch.

The reference methods are esterification–titration procedures. ISO 4629-1 acetylates the hydroxyl groups with acetic anhydride in pyridine without a catalyst and is written for binders that may carry hydroxyl as polyester end groups, partial esters or hydroxylated fatty acids; it excludes resins that also contain epoxy groups, which would be counted [3]. ISO 4629-2 uses a catalyst, applies to resins, binders, primary alcohols, glycols and fats, and leaves hydroxycarboxylic acids and phenolic hydroxyls to be decided case by case [6]. The former single-part ISO 4629 has been withdrawn and replaced by these two parts [3][6]. ASTM E222 offers three acetic-anhydride methods: a pressure-bottle method for general use, a reflux method, and a perchloric-acid-catalysed method for rapid or ambient-temperature work, which reads up to about 4 % higher than the other two [7]. ASTM D4274 adds five methods for polyester and polyether polyols, including imidazole-catalysed phthalic anhydride and pyromellitic dianhydride procedures [4]. Whichever is chosen, the result describes the sample at the moment it was drawn.

Kinetics: why the end point is slow to reach

Flory's 1939 study of the esterification of adipic acid with glycols, followed by titrating the unreacted carboxyl groups, showed that the reaction is third order when no catalyst is added, the carboxylic acid acting as its own catalyst, and second order in the presence of a strong acid catalyst, and that the rate constant does not fall as the chains grow longer, the basis of the equal-reactivity principle [8]. The same principle underlies the Carothers relation between conversion and chain length: the number-average degree of polymerisation equals the reciprocal of the fraction of unreacted functional groups, so a high molecular weight is reached only at very high conversion [5][9]. Three consequences follow for the plant. First, the acid value falls quickly early in the cook and slowly at the end, exactly where the specification is set. Second, the reaction is reversible and is driven by removing water, so the late-stage rate depends on vacuum and sparging as much as on chemistry [1]. Third, a titration that takes an hour or more reports a state that the reactor has already left, and the batch may be stopped late or early on the strength of it.

Why the reaction is visible in the near-infrared

The groups consumed and formed in a polyesterification all have near-infrared signatures. Workman and Weyer tabulate the overtone and combination bands of alcohol hydroxyls, of the hydroxyl in carboxylic acids, and of carbonyl compounds including esters [10]. In an in-reactor study of saturated polyester synthesis, Avila et al. found that the 1400–1500 nm region, assigned to the first overtone of the O–H stretch, carried most of the information for predicting acid number, while longer wavelengths helped mainly with outlier detection [1]. Because diol hydroxyl and carboxyl hydroxyl both absorb there, the spectrum changes as both end groups are consumed, and a multivariate calibration can separate the two contributions.

Marengo et al. modelled the hydroxyl number and acid value of 62 polyester resin samples from their FT-NIR spectra, compared back-propagation neural networks with PCR and PLS, and concluded that the acid and hydroxyl numbers of a dicarboxylic-acid–diol polyesterification can be predicted directly on the process line [11]. Avila et al. went into the reactor: a MEMS Fabry–Pérot sensor covering 1350–1650 nm, with an immersion transmission probe, followed 12 batches of saturated polyester resin in a two-litre reactor run under industrial conditions, a 24-hour process at 220–240 °C, and PLS models were built for acid number and viscosity alongside a PCA-based multivariate statistical process control model for end-point detection [1]. In the first reaction stage the acid number ranged from 5 to 20 mg KOH/g, and the authors note that part of the prediction scatter reflected the variability of the manual titration itself [1]. On the polyurethane side, Fage et al. used on-line NIR in a laboratory extruder to quantify isocyanate content from polyol–diisocyanate premixes, with at-line mid-infrared as verification [12], and Benali et al. followed the conversion of a polyurethane formulation through gelation with fibre-optic FT-NIR coupled to a rheometer [13].

Probe and calibration considerations

The in-reactor work also documents what makes the measurement hard. Avila et al. list temperature variation, solid particles and gas bubbles in a multiphase reaction fluid as disturbances that had to be handled by robust pre-processing and by long-term stability of the probe, and they state that calibration data must be representative of the process data the model will later see [1]. Three points follow:

  • Build the calibration from in-process spectra, not from finished resins alone. The spectra change in optical properties as the reaction proceeds, and the end-point model should span both reaction stages of a two-stage esterification [1].
  • The titration bounds the model. A calibration cannot be more accurate than the reference values behind it, so the acid and hydroxyl values should be determined by the standard procedures, with the same method throughout a project [2][3][6][7].
  • Treat the end point as a multivariate decision. A PCA-based control chart on the spectra can flag the end point independently of the predicted acid value and catches batches that drift from normal operation [1].
