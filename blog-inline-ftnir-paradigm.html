The Power of In-Line Analysis: A Paradigm Shift in Quality Control

What the shift from reactive, laboratory-based quality control to in-line measurement and control actually involves — the PAT framework, the ICH Q8/Q9/Q10 vocabulary, real-time release testing and multivariate control loops — as set out in the regulatory guidance and the process-analytics literature.

From reactive to proactive quality control

In the reactive model of quality control a sample is taken from the line, prepared and analysed, and the result arrives after the material it describes has moved on. In the chemical and pharmaceutical industries this reactive model was challenged formally in 2004, when the US Food and Drug Administration published its guidance on Process Analytical Technology (PAT), a framework in which quality is built into the process through timely measurement and control rather than verified afterwards by testing [1]. ICH Q8(R2) adopted the same principle: quality cannot be tested into products and should be built in by design [2]. Near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy is one of the measurement technologies named explicitly in this context [3].

What the PAT framework says

The FDA guidance defines PAT as a system for designing, analysing and controlling manufacturing through timely measurements, made during processing, of critical quality and performance attributes of raw and in-process materials and processes, with the goal of ensuring final product quality [1]. It distinguishes at-line measurement, where the sample is removed and analysed close to the process stream; on-line, where the sample is diverted and may be returned; and in-line, where the sample is not removed from the process stream at all [1]. It also warns that transferring a laboratory method to the line is not necessarily PAT: the point is process understanding, not the location of the instrument [1].

ICH Q8(R2) supplies the vocabulary: a critical quality attribute (CQA) is a property that should be kept within an appropriate limit, range or distribution; a design space is the demonstrated combination of input variables and process parameters that provides assurance of quality; and a control strategy is a planned set of controls, derived from product and process understanding, that ensures process performance and product quality [2]. ICH Q9(R1) provides the risk-management process used to decide which attributes and parameters matter [4], and ICH Q10 requires a monitoring system whose control strategy facilitates timely feedback and feedforward and verifies a state of control [5].

Real-time release testing

ICH Q8(R2) defines real time release testing (RTRT) as the ability to evaluate and ensure the quality of in-process and/or final product based on process data, typically a valid combination of measured material attributes and process controls; its example is unit-dose uniformity from in-process weight variation coupled with an NIR assay [2]. The European guideline on RTRT, in effect since October 2012, states that such a combination may provide greater assurance of product quality than end-product testing, and that RTRT will in general comprise process controls using PAT tools such as NIR and Raman spectroscopy, usually with multivariate analysis [3]. It also requires that if RTRT results fail or trend toward failure, the batch may not simply be released on end-product testing instead; the failure must be investigated [3].

Multivariate monitoring and control loops

Kourti explains why PAT and multivariate analysis belong together: real-time analysers provide timely data on quality properties, and combined with process data such as temperatures, flow rates and pressures they become a tool for process understanding, quality monitoring and abnormal-situation detection [6]. The statistical foundation predates the PAT initiative: MacGregor and Kourti showed in 1995 that multivariate charts based on Hotelling’s T² statistic handle many correlated variables, and that methods built on principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares (PLS) use both quality and process data for monitoring and fault diagnosis of continuous and batch processes [7].

A proactive loop closes when the measurement changes the process. ICH Q8(R2) contrasts a minimal approach, with in-process tests used for go/no-go decisions and off-line analysis, against an enhanced approach in which PAT tools are used with feed-forward and feedback controls and process operations are trended for continual improvement [2]. A multi-author review surveys sensors, monitoring and control methods and industrial applications [8], and FDA authors argue that advances in PAT and quality by design have brought the readiness needed for continuous manufacturing [9]. The literature also carries a caution: an industry consortium reports that as a process reaches commercial manufacture the monitoring technology is often simplified and in-situ PAT results are correlated with simple measurements such as temperature and pressure [10].

Putting an in-line NIR analyser to work

Bakeev’s reference volume on PAT covers the spectroscopic techniques, sampling, chemometrics and implementation strategies for chemical and pharmaceutical processes [11]. Three practical points follow from the guidance documents:

  • Representative measurement. The FDA notes that collecting representative samples has its own complexities — powder sampling, for example, can be erroneous — so the probe location and sampling interface need as much attention as the spectrometer [1].
  • Validation against the laboratory. For a new process analyser, a test-to-test comparison with the conventional method on collected samples may be the only available validation option [1].
  • Risk assessment and data trends. The FDA recommends a risk analysis before an experimental analyser is installed, and notes that new measurement tools may reveal data trends intrinsic to an acceptable process, which should be evaluated scientifically [1][4].
