MEMS Comb-Drive Mechanical Durability in Industrial Environments

What the published reliability studies say about micromachined silicon springs and comb drives under vibration, shock, temperature cycling and cyclic fatigue, what MIL-STD-810H and the IEC 60068-2 test methods actually specify, and how an industrial analyzer specification should use them.

Why the question comes up

Inline analyzers in the food and feed industry are mounted on conveyors, mills, blenders and discharge chutes, so any moving optical element inside them lives with continuous vibration, occasional shock and wide temperature swings. Micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) put such moving elements — mirrors, shutters, resonators — on a silicon chip, and a plant engineer reasonably asks whether a structure a few micrometres thick survives there. Silicon was adopted as a mechanical material because of its mechanical properties and because sensors can be batch-fabricated from it [1]; at room temperature it is brittle and fractures rather than yields [2]. The electrostatic comb drive used in many of these devices was introduced by Tang et al.: interdigitated finger structures move a polysilicon shuttle parallel to the substrate on folded-beam springs, and the first devices resonated between 18 and 80 kHz [3]. Because such a suspension flexes but does not slide, the durability question is one of fracture, fatigue, stiction and packaging rather than of bearing wear.

What the Sandia reliability programme measured

The most complete public dataset on silicon micromechanisms under environmental stress is the Sandia National Laboratories report by Tanner et al., which tested surface-micromachined microengines (comb-drive actuators on springs, driving a gear) in humidity, temperature, shock, vibration and storage experiments [4]. Its central finding is that the dominant failure mechanism for operating devices was wear of polysilicon rubbing surfaces, which the authors generalise to any MEMS device with rubbing or closely contacting surfaces [4]. A spring-suspended comb drive without rubbing contact therefore avoids the main wear mechanism, but not stiction, which the report attributes to capillary forces that are large at these dimensions and which is countered with hydrophobic coatings or supercritical drying [4].

The environmental results are specific. In shock tests with haversine pulses of 0.2 to 1 ms from 500 g to 40,000 g on unpowered devices, all 24 microengines at 500 g and 22 at 1,000 g functioned afterwards with no damage; above 4,000 g debris from the die edges moved and caused shorts, at 10,000 g the die-attach bond failed, and damage to thin flexures and 2.5 µm pin joints first appeared at 20,000 g [4]. In random vibration from 20 to 2,000 Hz at 0.8 g²/Hz (about 40 g rms, peak around 120 g, three minutes per axis, unpowered), chosen as roughly four times the system requirement, 17 of 22 microengines functioned afterwards, and the three electrical failures also appeared in the unstressed control group [4]. In temperature cycling, all 20 devices remained functional after three cycles between −55 and +75 °C and after four further cycles between −55 and +200 °C, and a thermal-expansion model indicated that expansion of the polysilicon parts is not a significant failure mechanism [4]. Srikar and Senturia explain the shock tolerance: for shock durations of 50–5,000 µs the substrate responds as a rigid body and is expected to be immune to stress-wave damage, and whether a microstructure responds in the impulse, resonant or quasi-static regime is decided by the shock duration relative to its natural period [5].

Fatigue: silicon is not immune at the micron scale

The assumption that a brittle material cannot fatigue does not hold for thin silicon films. Muhlstein et al. cycled notched single-crystal silicon cantilevers about 20 µm thick in air at 30 °C and 50 % relative humidity and observed failure after 106 to 1011 cycles at stress amplitudes of roughly 4 to 10 GPa [6]. Kahn et al., reviewing the field, note that the phenomenon was first detected in 1992, that lifetime increases as peak stress decreases, that it depends on the cyclic stress level but not on cycle frequency, and that the mechanism remains debated [2]. Pierron and Muhlstein showed on deep-reactive-ion-etched films that the rate of damage accumulation is strongly sensitive to stress amplitude and, more importantly, to humidity, with faster accumulation in 50 % relative-humidity air than in 25 % air or medium vacuum [7]. Alsem et al. summarise the literature as consistently showing longer life at lower cyclic stress and argue that a reaction-layer mechanism in the surface oxide best explains why micron-scale silicon fatigues while bulk silicon does not [8].

The engineering reading is straightforward. A spring that operates at a small fraction of its fracture strength sits in the long-life region of these stress-life curves, and a dry, sealed package slows the humidity-driven part of the damage accumulation [7][8]. What matters for a particular device is the stress amplitude in its springs at the design deflection and the atmosphere inside the package, so fatigue margins are assessed per design rather than quoted as a property of “silicon” [2][8].

What the environmental test standards actually specify

MIL-STD-810 is the United States Department of Defense test method standard for environmental engineering considerations and laboratory tests; revision H was issued in January 2019 and Change 1 in May 2022 [9]. It is often misread as a pass/fail certificate. The standard states that it does not impose design or test specifications but describes a tailoring process in which methods and severities are derived from the materiel's expected service life, and it provides individual methods such as 501.7 (high temperature), 502.7 (low temperature), 514.8 (vibration) and 516.8 (shock) for that purpose [9]. A claim that a product was “tested to MIL-STD-810H” is therefore only meaningful together with the method, procedure and profile used [9].

The IEC 60068-2 series serves the same purpose for electrical and electronic equipment and is the usual basis for industrial instrument qualification. IEC 60068-2-6 (Test Fc) determines the ability of a specimen to withstand specified severities of sinusoidal vibration and to reveal mechanical weakness or degradation of specified performance [10]. IEC 60068-2-27 (Test Ea) covers non-repetitive and repetitive shocks and is intended to reveal mechanical weakness, degradation or accumulated damage caused by shock [11]. IEC 60068-2-64 (Test Fh) applies broadband random vibration, the stochastic excitation actually seen in transport and operational environments, and is used to identify accumulated stress effects [12]. Ingress protection is a separate matter: IEC 60529 defines the IP code that classifies the degrees of protection provided by enclosures for electrical equipment [13]. When specifying an inline analyzer, three points follow:

  • Ask for the method and the severity, not the label. Both MIL-STD-810H and IEC 60068-2 define procedures whose levels are set by the relevant specification or by tailoring, so a test report should state the method, the profile (frequency range, amplitude or spectral density, duration) and whether the instrument was powered and measuring during the test [9][10][11][12].
  • Match the test to the mounting point. Random-vibration severity is defined as a spectral density over a frequency band [12], so the vibration at the intended mounting position should be characterised and compared with the qualification profile before installation.
  • Separate the optics from the enclosure. The shock and vibration tolerance of a silicon microstructure and the IP rating of the housing are different qualifications under different standards [4][13]; neither implies the other.
