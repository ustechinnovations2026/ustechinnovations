NIR vs. FT-NIR: What’s the Difference — and Why It Matters for Inline Process Control

How scanning-grating, diode-array and Fourier-transform near-infrared analyzers produce a spectrum, what the Jacquinot, Fellgett and laser-referenced wavelength advantages really buy and where they stop, and which trade-offs decide an inline installation — based on the instrumentation literature rather than on vendor claims.

Why the architecture question comes up

Near-infrared analyzers used in the food and feed industry, in dairy and in chemical and pharmaceutical plants are built around several different optical designs. Pasquini's review of NIR instrumentation groups them as filter instruments, scanning grating monochromators, fixed gratings with photodiode arrays, acousto-optic tunable filters and Fourier-transform (FT) interferometers [1]. Benchtop laboratory spectrometers have converged on the FT design, whereas process and portable instruments still use the whole range of principles [2]. These are coexisting engineering choices with different trade-offs, not successive “generations”, and the handbook literature treats dispersive and FT instruments side by side [10]. The question for an inline project is therefore not which architecture is better, but which trade-offs matter for the measurement at hand.

How each architecture produces a spectrum

A scanning grating monochromator rotates a grating so that one narrow wavelength band at a time reaches a single detector. It is the lowest-cost scanning design, but Pasquini lists a lack of wavelength precision that deteriorates in long-term operation and moving parts that limit its use in harsh environments [1]. A diode-array instrument keeps the grating fixed and places a linear PbS or InGaAs array of up to 256 elements in its focal plane, so that a complete spectrum is recorded in a few milliseconds with no moving parts, which is why this design is used for field and truck-mounted analyzers [1]. Beć et al. describe the same multichannel principle as measuring all wavelengths simultaneously without any scanning, with high mechanical resistance and compact dimensions [2].

An FT-NIR instrument passes the whole broadband beam through an interferometer, records an interferogram as the optical path difference changes and reconstructs the spectrum by a Fourier transform; benchtop instruments use either a Michelson or a polarization interferometer [2]. Pasquini credits the FT design with the best wavelength accuracy and precision of the NIR architectures, better than 0.05 nm, a resolution below 1 nm and a high signal-to-noise ratio, but also notes that it scans more slowly than an acousto-optic filter and is less robust, although vibration-tolerant interferometer designs exist [1].

Throughput, multiplex and wavelength-axis advantages — and their limits

Three classical advantages are claimed for interferometers. The Jacquinot (throughput) advantage goes back to Jacquinot's 1954 comparison of prisms, gratings and Fabry-Perot etalons at equal resolving power and equal size, which found interference instruments far more luminous than gratings [4]. An interferometer has no entrance or exit slits, so more of the source radiation reaches the detector at a given resolution [1][3]. Griffiths and de Haseth point out, however, that the gain actually realised is bounded by the size of the detector and by the optics between source, sample and detector [3]; with a fibre-optic probe or a diffuse-reflectance interface, the sampling optics rather than the spectrometer often set the throughput.

The Fellgett (multiplex) advantage is conditional. When the measurement is limited by detector noise, recording all spectral elements at once improves the signal-to-noise ratio, for the same measuring time, by roughly the square root of the number of resolution elements compared with a scanning single-channel instrument; when the noise is proportional to the square root of the signal (shot-noise limited) the advantage vanishes, and when the noise is proportional to the signal, multiplexing becomes a disadvantage because noise from strong spectral regions is spread over weak ones [3]. Which regime applies depends on the detector, the source and the light level at the detector and has to be examined per instrument. The comparison also only concerns scanning designs: a diode-array instrument records all wavelengths simultaneously as well [2], so between FT and array instruments the multiplex argument does not arise and the discussion moves to throughput, resolution, the wavelength axis and robustness.

The wavelength-axis (Connes) advantage is the most robust of the three: the interferogram is sampled at the fringes of a reference laser, so the wavenumber scale is tied to the laser wavelength [3]; Beć et al. name the precisely controlled wavelength axis, with the throughput gain, as the primary benefits of the FT principle [2]. This does not remove the need for verification. ASTM E1944 defines level-zero and level-one performance tests specifically for laboratory FT-NIR spectrometers [11], and the USP chapter on NIR theory and practice addresses both FT instruments, which report in wavenumber, and dispersive instruments, which report in wavelength [13].

Spectral range, resolution and what comparisons show

Spectral range is largely a detector question rather than an architecture question. Standard InGaAs detectors perform best at around 1000–1600 nm, while extended InGaAs reaches longer wavelengths at the cost of lower sensitivity and, often, cooling [2]; the combination bands lie in the 1600–2500 nm region, and PbS detectors and arrays are used to reach it [1]. An array instrument fitted with a PbS or extended-InGaAs array therefore covers the combination region, and an FT instrument fitted with a standard InGaAs detector does not; the claim that only interferometers can reach the long-wavelength NIR is not supported by the instrumentation literature [1][2]. Resolution is likewise application-dependent: NIR spectra of liquids and solids are dominated by broad overtone and combination bands [1], and the sub-nanometre resolution available from an interferometer is an advantage only where the application resolves such detail.

Direct comparisons bear this out. Sinnaeve et al. analysed raw apple juices on a dispersive NIR instrument in transmittance, the same instrument in transflectance, an FT-NIR instrument with a fibre-optic transmittance probe and an FT-MIR instrument, and found the best results with FT-MIR and with dispersive NIR transmittance, while transflectance and the FT-NIR configuration were less accurate [6]. The sampling interface and the measurement mode mattered at least as much as the spectrometer principle. Workman's review of calibration transfer reaches a related conclusion: obtaining identical results from two instruments with one calibration still eludes practitioners, and the reasons lie in instrument reproducibility, reference values, mathematics and sample presentation, not in one architecture alone [5]. Beć et al. list the operating spectral region, wavelength accuracy, dynamic range, sample-to-optics distance and detector photometric response as the instrument differences most likely to invalidate a transferred model [2].

What matters for inline use

For an inline installation the decisive questions are usually mechanical and operational rather than spectroscopic, and three deserve attention before any architecture is chosen:

  • Moving parts, vibration and temperature. Array instruments have no moving parts and tolerate mechanical stress [1][2]; a Michelson interferometer has a moving mirror and depends on vibration-tolerant engineering [1]. Miniaturised and MEMS-based interferometers exist, but Beć et al. show that miniaturisation introduces trade-offs that reduce the advantage of the FT principle and that no single design has emerged as best among compact instruments [2][7][8].
  • The sampling interface. Probe type, path length, window material and measurement mode shape the result as strongly as the spectrometer [6]; fibre runs and diffuse-reflectance optics also decide how much of a throughput advantage survives [3].
  • Validation and calibration transfer. ASTM D6122 sets out how the performance of online, at-line and laboratory infrared analyzers using multivariate models is validated, from instrument checks through model applicability to agreement with the reference method [12]; whether a laboratory calibration can be moved to the inline instrument is a calibration-transfer question that applies to every architecture [5].

Pasquini's 2018 review frames the field as a mature technique whose renewed interest comes from innovative, often portable, instrumentation and from chemometrics, while warning about misuse of the mathematical tools [9]; Crocombe's review of portable spectroscopy documents the breadth of engines now available, from MEMS interferometers to digital-micromirror and linear-variable-filter designs [7]. The practical reading is that FT and dispersive NIR each have installations where they are the better fit, and that the fit is established by testing on the actual process stream.

How USTECH approaches the choice

USTECH offers both architectures and selects per application. The ProLine2550 is an inline FT-NIR analyzer for blenders, reactors and pipelines; the ProLine17EC and ProLine17ECS are rugged inline NIR analyzers built on an InGaAs photodiode array with no moving parts, and the MasterLine17EC is a benchtop and at-line NIR analyzer for grains, feed ingredients and forage. Calibrations for either family are developed and transferred in caliX, following the transfer practices reviewed by Workman [5]. Which spectrometer principle, spectral range and sampling interface suit a given line is established in a feasibility study on that line's own samples and reference analyses.
