Multiplicative Scatter Correction and Standard Normal Variate Preprocessing

What multiplicative scatter correction (MSC) and the standard normal variate transform (SNV) actually do to a near-infrared spectrum, how the two are mathematically related, where they differ in practice and what can go wrong — drawn from the original papers and the follow-up literature.

Why scatter correction comes first

Diffuse-reflectance NIR spectra of powders, grains and meals in the food and feed industry carry, on top of the chemical absorption signal, physical effects that change from sample to sample. Barnes et al. describe the combined multiplicative effects of particle size and light scatter as the main obstacle to interpreting such spectra, and note that particle size accounts for most of the spectral variance while chemical composition contributes comparatively little [1]. Rinnan et al. divide the common NIR pre-processing methods into scatter corrections (MSC, SNV, normalisation) and spectral derivatives, and compare their effects on real data [3]. This guide covers the first family; derivatives are treated in the Savitzky-Golay guide.

Multiplicative scatter correction (MSC)

MSC was introduced by Geladi, MacDougall and Martens in 1985, for NIR reflectance spectra of meat, as a multi-wavelength method that separates chemical absorption from physical scatter, the latter modelled as an additive plus a multiplicative component [2]. Each spectrum xi is regressed, over all wavelengths, on a reference spectrum xref: xi ≈ ai + bi·xref; the corrected spectrum is (xi − ai)/bi. The reference is in practice the mean spectrum of the calibration set, which is why Dhanoa et al. call MSC a set-dependent transformation [4]. In the meat study, MSC combined with a linearising transform such as corrected log(1/R) or inverse Kubelka-Munk gave the best fat predictions and simpler calibration models [2]. Isaksson and Næs then tested MSC on five food products with principal component regression for protein, fat, water and carbohydrate, and found that it lowered the prediction error in every regression, by 7 % to 68 % relative to the uncorrected absorbance spectra [5].

The set dependence has a practical consequence: the reference spectrum is part of the calibration model, must be applied unchanged to every new spectrum, and a change of reference after recalibration on a new population changes every corrected spectrum [4].

Standard normal variate (SNV) and de-trending

Barnes, Dhanoa and Lister proposed SNV in 1989 as a transformation applied to each spectrum individually: the spectrum's own mean over all wavelengths is subtracted and the result divided by its own standard deviation, which removes the multiplicative effects of scatter and particle size without reference to any other sample [1]. In the same paper they introduced de-trending, in which a second-degree polynomial in wavelength is fitted to the SNV-corrected spectrum and subtracted to remove the baseline shift and curvature they describe as common in powdered or densely packed samples; they present the result as an alternative to derivative spectra, whose shapes they regard as harder to interpret [1]. Because SNV uses only the spectrum being corrected, it is set-independent: no reference has to be chosen, stored or transferred.

How MSC and SNV are related, and how they differ

Dhanoa et al. showed that the set-dependent MSC and the set-independent SNV transformations of a spectrum are linearly related, as theory predicts; the link needs only the mean and standard deviation of the set-mean spectrum and the correlation coefficient between the individual spectrum and that set mean, and it is through these three quantities that set dependence enters MSC [4]. They conclude that the two are alternative routes to reducing particle-size effects and are interconvertible [4]. For a homogeneous calibration set the two usually give similar spectra and similar models, which is why they are often treated as interchangeable.

They are not identical, however. Fearn et al. examined the geometry of the two transformations in spectral space and reported two distinct behaviours: SNV can induce curved structures in score plots of the treated spectra, while MSC tends to produce outliers; both effects were illustrated with NIR imaging camera data and explained geometrically [6]. A curved band in a PCA score plot after SNV, or a few extreme samples after MSC, may therefore be an artefact of the pre-processing rather than a chemical feature, and should be checked before it is interpreted or used to reject samples [6]. A second pitfall is shared by both: they assume that the chemical signal is a small part of the spectrum and that the physical effects are the same at every wavelength. When the analyte dominates the spectral variation, the correction can remove part of the analyte signal; Martens, Nielsen and Engelsen found exactly this for standard MSC on transmission spectra of wheat gluten and starch powder mixtures, where it removed too much analyte information [7].

Extended MSC

Extended multiplicative signal correction (EMSC) was proposed by Martens and Stark in 1991 to bring prior knowledge into pre-processing: it removes the effects of uncontrolled path length and light scattering, and its companion, spectral interference subtraction, removes known interferents; both were demonstrated on toluene in benzene–xylene mixtures with simulated, substantial scattering effects [8]. Martens, Nielsen and Engelsen later extended the model to correct additive, multiplicative and wavelength-dependent scattering effects together; after EMSC their gluten–starch powder spectra behaved much like spectra of transparent solutions and followed Beer's law [7]. EMSC is the method to consider when plain MSC or SNV visibly distorts the analyte bands, or when the scatter effect is known to vary with wavelength.

Choosing between them in practice

There is no single correct choice; the literature supports a short procedure rather than a default:

  • Decide with the model, not by eye. Treat MSC, SNV, SNV with de-trending and EMSC as candidate steps, combine each with the derivative setting under consideration, and compare them by cross-validation error, as Rinnan et al. do for the common methods [3].
  • Keep the reference with the model. An MSC reference spectrum must be applied unchanged at prediction time; if the calibration population is likely to change, SNV avoids the set dependence altogether [4].
  • Inspect the scores after correction. Curved structures after SNV and new outliers after MSC are known behaviours of the transforms, not necessarily of the samples [6]; if the analyte dominates the spectrum, consider EMSC [7].

Scatter correction in caliX

In the caliX suite MSC, SNV, de-trending and derivative filters are configured in the pre-processing step of model development, where candidate combinations are compared by cross-validation before a PLS model is fixed and the MSC reference spectrum is stored with the model. The same workflow serves spectra from the MasterLine17EC at-line analyzer for grains and feed ingredients and from the ProLine17EC inline analyzer, where particle size and packing vary with the process. Which correction suits a given material is settled in a feasibility study with your own samples.
