Real-Time Moisture Control in Corn Gluten Feed Dryers

Why the gluten feed dryer is one of the largest energy consumers in a corn wet mill, what over-drying and under-drying cost in energy and product quality, and what the published literature says about measuring moisture on-line with near-infrared spectroscopy and using it to control a dryer.

Why dryer moisture matters in a wet mill

Corn wet milling separates the kernel into starch, germ, fibre and gluten; fibre, steep liquor and gluten are sold into the feed industry as corn gluten feed and corn gluten meal [1]. Corn gluten feed is worth about the same as, or less than, the incoming corn, so its margin depends on what it costs to make [1]. Dewatering, evaporation and drying dominate the energy use of a wet mill, and energy is its second largest operating cost after the corn itself [2]. The gluten feed dryer therefore sits between the fuel bill and the moisture specification of a co-product sold by weight.

What is being dried

The fibre is dewatered mechanically to roughly 65–75 % moisture in a screen centrifuge and about ten percentage points further in a screw press; steep liquor is then added and the mixture is dried to corn gluten feed, most commonly in a direct-fired, co-current rotary dryer, or in steam-tube dryers where steam is cheaper [2]. The dryer feed is not constant: in a 22-week dairy trial, nutrient concentrations of delivered wet and dry corn gluten feed varied throughout, and the authors recommended sampling every new delivery [3]. Water removal is a recognised cost problem of corn co-products in general [4].

Both directions of error have a price. Wet corn gluten feed stored without spoilage only at 6 °C; at 17 or 26.5 °C a preservative was needed to hold it for two to three weeks [5], which is why the product is dried before shipping [2]. In the other direction, heat damages protein: in distillers dried grains with solubles heat-treated at 130 °C, the standardised ileal digestibility of lysine in pigs fell from about 67 % to 52–55 % [6]. Corn gluten feed is a different co-product, but it is dried in the same class of equipment, and the ENERGY STAR guide notes that rotary dryers can scorch the product [2].

Energy, over-drying and the control problem

In the ENERGY STAR energy balance for a modern 100,000 bushel per day plant, the gluten feed dryer is listed at 25,419 Btu per bushel of primary energy out of 115,777 for the whole plant, roughly 22 % [2]. Every point of moisture removed below the target is water evaporated for nothing and product weight lost at the scale. The guide's remedy is explicit: monitoring outlet temperature and moisture and controlling dryer feed conditions and temperature may save energy and narrow the range of product dryness, and model predictive control was already being applied to dryers in corn wet milling [2]. Over-drying is a generic rotary-dryer problem: an intelligent controller on a cut-tobacco rotary dryer, designed to hold outlet moisture at its optimum, reduced over-dried product compared with regulatory control [7]. The loop is only as good as its measurement, however: a fuzzy controller on a fertiliser rotary dryer cut biomass fuel use by 9 % by holding exhaust-gas temperature at its optimum, but the plant had no on-line moisture instrument, so temperature stood in for the variable that actually mattered [8].

Measuring moisture: reference method and NIR

The reference method for moisture in feed materials is the oven-drying loss method of ISO 6496, a laboratory procedure that reports hours after sampling, long after the dryer conditions have changed [9]. Near-infrared spectrometry is the rapid alternative, and ISO 12099 gives the guidelines for calibrating, validating and monitoring NIR instruments on animal feeding stuffs [10]. For corn co-products, a 2025 study with 30 PLS models on 1,437 reference samples including DDGS reported moisture models generally above a ratio of performance to deviation of 3 on held-out data, while noting that material heterogeneity co-determined performance [11].

Moving the measurement to the dryer outlet has its own literature. Green et al., monitoring fluid bed dryers by in-line NIR at three scales, showed that process heterogeneity plays a major role in apparent prediction accuracy and that the method with the lowest apparent error is not always the best choice for control [12]. Bogomolov et al. found that turbulent material movement past the probe produces intensity fluctuations that look spectrally like scatter, so conventional scatter correction also removes moisture information; averaging spectra over time brought the in-line root-mean-square error to about 0.1 % moisture, while probe fouling had only a minor effect [13]. These are pharmaceutical results, but the mechanisms — a moving, heterogeneous solid in front of a probe and sparse, delayed reference values — apply to a co-product dryer too.

Putting an NIR analyzer on a dryer

  • Sample where the product is representative. Apparent accuracy depends on how the material is presented to the probe [12], so the measuring point must see a well-mixed stream, and calibration reference samples must be taken there at the same time.
  • Calibrate on the plant's own material and keep validating. Corn gluten feed composition shifts between deliveries [3]; the calibration population must span those shifts and be checked against ISO 6496 results [9] as ISO 12099 describes [10].
  • Treat the NIR value as the controlled variable, not a display. The documented energy benefit comes from controlling the dryer on outlet moisture [2]; a controller acting only on exhaust temperature is using a proxy [8].
