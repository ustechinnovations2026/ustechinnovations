Moisture and Fat Standardization in Pet Food Extrusion

Why the moisture of kibble leaving the dryer and the fat applied in the coater decide storage stability and label compliance in dry pet food, what the reference methods and regulations require, and what peer-reviewed NIR studies on extruded pet food show about measuring them.

Why moisture and fat decide the fate of a kibble

Most dry pet food in the food and feed industry is made by extrusion cooking, which gelatinises starch and improves digestibility and palatability at the cost of some heat-labile vitamins and Maillard losses [1]. The recipe is extruded at 23 to 28 % moisture, loses water by flash evaporation at the die, and is then dried, because the final moisture of dry-expanded products needs to be below 10 % to prevent mould and bacterial growth, with 12 % as the maximum for storage stability [2]. The underlying variable is water activity, for which Rokey et al. give limits of about 0.65 for bacteria and 0.6 for moulds [2]; Beuchat's review explains why fungal spoilage dominates at water activities of 0.61 to 0.85, where competing bacteria are absent [3]. The risk is real: of three extruded dog diets made on the same extruder, one had moulded after eight months of controlled storage, which the authors attribute to inaccurate measurement of its moisture content after production [4].

Moisture and fat are also label declarations. In the United States the analysis panel required under the AAFCO model regulations carries four basic declarations: minimum crude protein, minimum crude fat, maximum crude fibre and maximum moisture [5]. In the European Union, Regulation (EC) No 767/2009 requires complete feed for cats and dogs to declare crude protein, crude fibres, crude fat and crude ash, and moisture must be stated whenever it exceeds 14 % [6]. The economics pull the other way: ingredients arrive at 10 to 13 % moisture while finished feeds sell at 8 to 10 %, so over-drying is weight the plant has paid for and does not sell [2].

The reference methods behind the numbers

Moisture in feed is determined by the oven-drying method of ISO 6496, which covers compound feeds but excludes, among others, milk products and mineral feeds [7]. Fat is determined by ISO 6492, which sorts feeds into two categories and requires acid hydrolysis before solvent extraction for the category that includes products of animal origin [8]. For extrudates this step is not a formality: extrusion at low moisture and high temperature forms lipid–starch and lipid–protein complexes that Soxhlet extraction alone cannot break down, so fat analysis of extruded products must include acid hydrolysis [2]. A NIR fat calibration is only as meaningful as its reference values, so a plant has to decide which fat definition it is calibrating to — hydrolysed total fat or extractable fat — and apply it consistently before and after coating.

Where fat enters the line

Fat usually makes up less than 20 % of a complete recipe [2]. Part of it is added in the preconditioner, where it coats the particles and hinders moisture uptake and heat transfer, so high-fat formulas need longer preconditioning to reach the same degree of cook [2]. The remainder goes on after drying: most dry-expanded products are coated with liquid fat and palatants after drying, cooling and sizing, often onto a still-warm product to improve absorption, and when more than five to eight percent fat is applied the product is dried, coated and only then cooled [2]. Vacuum infusion coaters draw the liquid into the cell structure of the kibble and permit far higher liquid additions than atmospheric spraying [2]. The coating is also where shelf life is decided: in a storage study of kibble coated at 10 % with ten lipid sources, lipid source was the dominant determinant of oxidative stability, with the kibble matrix contributing as well [9]. The fat the coater applies has to match a kibble whose density and porosity are set by moisture and expansion upstream, which is why the two are best controlled together.

What NIR can and cannot do on extruded pet food

ISO 12099 is the guideline for applying near-infrared spectrometry to animal feeding stuffs, including how calibrations are validated and monitored against the reference methods [10]. The largest published pet-food data set is that of De Marchi et al., who measured 649 commercial extruded dog foods, unground, on a visible/near-infrared instrument covering 570 to 1,842 nm and obtained cross-validation coefficients of determination of 0.77 for moisture, 0.97 for crude protein, 0.83 for crude fat, 0.86 for crude fibre and 0.78 for ash, while the peroxide-value model (0.66) was judged fit for screening only [11]. Goi et al. showed that at-line visible/NIR spectroscopy on ground kibble predicts total and gelatinised starch with residual predictive deviations above 4 in external validation, so degree of cook is an accessible process indicator as well [12].

Two cautions follow. The moderate moisture statistic of De Marchi et al. comes from a population spanning hundreds of product types measured through intact kibble [11]; a single line running a few recipes inside a narrow moisture window is a different calibration problem, and performance there has to be established on that line's own samples. And the lower accuracy for moisture and ash on unground samples [11] is a reminder that kibble size, surface fat and temperature all shape the spectrum: a probe after the coater sees a fat film rather than the kibble interior, so a calibration built on dried, uncoated product should not be assumed to transfer unchanged.

Measurement points and working practice

Rokey et al. already noted continuous on-line moisture meters and called monitoring of final-product moisture essential for stability during storage [2]. In an extrusion line the measurement points that repay the effort are:

  • Dryer exit, before coating. Here moisture is set against the storage limit [2] and the label maximum [5][6], an uncoated kibble gives the cleanest spectrum, and dryer temperature, bed depth or retention time can still be corrected.
  • After the coater. Total fat and, where calibrated, moisture of the finished kibble; reference samples should use the acid-hydrolysis fat method [8], and the model is validated separately from the dryer-exit model.
  • A reference routine per ISO 12099. Periodic samples analysed by ISO 6496 and ISO 6492 [7][8], bias and slope checks, and documented recalibration when recipes change [10].
